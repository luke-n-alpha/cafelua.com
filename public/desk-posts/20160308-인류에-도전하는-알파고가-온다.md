**4. 인공지능은 바둑 말고도 더 할 수 있다.**

**2. What is AlphaGo?**

If you search for AlphaGo, you'll find about four terms: Monte Carlo tree search, deep learning, value network, and policy network. I'll briefly(?) explain these four terms. First, Monte Carlo tree search is simply about considering possibilities. If I place a stone here, my opponent will place one like this, right? Then what about next? And next? And next? If you draw this, it forms a tree shape, and following this path to compare options is what computers call 'search.' You can just think of Monte Carlo as one of many search methods. It's simply like finding a path. Second is deep learning, one of the learning methods for AI. This algorithm once surprised people by autonomously learning to recognize cat photos. It's particularly good at recognizing images, adept at seeing shapes distorted or examining parts individually. If it were to identify an elephant, it would be like looking at it from a distance, then examining only its legs up close, then only its trunk. Through this algorithm, AlphaGo reads the board. It determines whether the local situation is advantageous or disadvantageous, or whether the broader situation is advantageous or disadvantageous. For the third and fourth terms, policy network and value network, forget the 'network' part. While specialists in this field might have a different understanding of the term 'network,' it's unrelated to the communication network you generally know. It might be easier to think of them as models or knowledge. The policy network reads the next move by looking at the probability distribution of possible next moves. It's like, 'If I play here, my opponent will play here next, won't they?' The value network assesses the board position. It's like, 'Given the result of my move here, my winning probability is X percent, and if my opponent plays here next, my winning probability is Y percent.' The policy network helps determine which options to explore first during probability search, and the value network checks if the explored content was well-found. **(Please point out any errors. **3. AlphaGo, the Go Enthusiast. Weak AI**

However, as you can see from the explanation above, AlphaGo is an AI created solely to play Go well. It's an artificial modeling of the rules, skills, and knowledge inherent in Go. This friend has only ever seen Go and only knows Go. If you want it to play Omok (Gomoku), you'd have to teach it Omok again. Of course, Omok would be faster since the rules are simpler. What about Korean chess (Janggi)? Again, you'd have to teach it from scratch. This friend continuously thinks about better moves, and if you teach it something new, it will again continuously think about the best moves for that. However, it can't even dream of how to increase its CPU to think of better moves. It doesn't complain about low power either. This is why this friend cannot become Skynet. Furthermore, if you teach this friend basic addition or subtraction, which ordinary computers do well, it might even get them wrong. This is because it chooses what is probabilistically high, not what is semantically accurate. It's similar to how Google's translator, which is based on statistics rather than linguistic analysis, can accurately translate commonly used phrases and even internet slang, but might get less common, precise dictionary meanings wrong. Like humans, computer AI is biased by what it has seen and heard. It cannot even judge when to use the accuracy that computers possess. In other words, AlphaGo is just a Go enthusiast. Such weak AI can effectively mimic the reasoning of human brain activity for specific purposes. But again, this friend cannot become Skynet. While it would be nice if it could evolve on its own, it's still a friend that requires a lot of hands-on work from programmers. For Lee Se-dol 9-dan, it's merely reached the point where playing a single-player Go game can finally be enjoyable. If AI were too foolish, the difficulty would be too easy, making the game boring. The truly greater meaning of AlphaGo is that even Go, which humans considered difficult and excelled at, can be surpassed by a machine if engineers are deployed. **It means that software created by dozens of engineers can be better than one genius.** This implies that it can apply to almost all intellectual fields. Therefore, the talk of job threats is clearly a reality. If Lee Se-dol 9-dan loses, perhaps a separate AI competition will emerge in future Go world championships. It might turn into an AI vs. AI showdown. This year, will the winner be MS or Google? Something like that?

**4. AI Can Do More Than Just Go.**

The greater potential of AI also includes the implementation of the Semantic Web and emotion recognition. Much of the unstructured data stored on the web is not ideal for machine learning. While technologies like big data exist, if meaning is well-structured, learning can be much faster. If content is well-created from the production stage, or if unstructured data can be continuously converted into a structured Semantic Web, then AI trained on this could evolve from a highly accurate search engine into something that truly possesses all human knowledge. And based on this, it might even be possible to create something akin to a self. Then, a true singularity might occur. In the case of emotion recognition, it could simply create more value by making users feel better, but conversely, it could also deceive. After all, fraudsters often appear the most benevolent. Ultimately, AI, with its powerful capabilities, will require moral and social consensus. Whether we like it or not, we have now entered an era where we live alongside machines that surpass human physical and intellectual abilities in various fields. While it's uncertain when the singularity will be reached, AI will pose unprecedented questions about labor, which we have always considered both a right and a duty.
