Recently, it seems criticism of VR has become quite serious, so I'd like to offer a few words. While it's true there were excessive expectations for VR, I want to discuss claims that VR is failing like 3D TV, or that VR is already over and AR is the future, in response to these fading expectations. First, regarding all the aforementioned excessive expectations, it's certainly true that compared to the immense expectations when Facebook was sold for 2 trillion won in 2014, VR has not become a golden goose. But is VR really failing? Will it follow the path of 3D TV? Without even bringing up the 5G issue, which could be mistaken for a mere trend, I will offer a simple counter-argument as to why it's not.

Another point is the reaction of children and teenagers. While investors and decision-makers are generally older, VR is not unfamiliar to children and teenagers. It's simply a device they can't afford because it's expensive. I was truly surprised when I went to test Vtime, a social VR, and met middle school students playing with VR-boxes. And then, the subsequent VR restrictions for children... (I, too, loved comics and computers from an early age and grew up hearing constantly that they were bad. Industries that children love and adults worry about are bound to be future industries.) The growth of webtoons and games in Korea was actually backed by the growth of a demographic accustomed to digital media consumption. Naver Webtoon's 'chogling' (elementary schoolers) won't be 'chogling' forever. While their purchasing power is weaker than those in their 30s and 40s, the purchasing rate of digital content by those in their 20s cannot be ignored, and the latent purchasing power of teenagers explodes. Those who currently prefer VR still have weak purchasing power. Despite weak purchasing power, there are markets like Southeast Asia that generate tremendous responses even from cheap VR like Cardboard.

**3) Steady technological advancement**
Although Google is said to have abandoned Daydream (looking at the patents, they haven't abandoned it, and as a technology-based company, they'll approach it in ways other than just products), Facebook, on the other hand, continues to invest. And at Connect 6, they began to drastically reduce the size of VR HMDs and introduced a remote-free, hand-based UX using AI. They also made wireless VR HMDs directly compatible with Rift. All these technological directions point to one thing: "lightness, improved accessibility." Of course, Korea is not leading this technology market, but once it's complete, they'll surely make and sell it well.

**2. Counter-argument to 'VR is out, AR is in'**
**This seems to be one of the claims frequently seen these days: that VR is out and AR is in. It feels like AR and VR are being viewed as competing technologies, so I'd like to clarify a few points.**

**1) VR and AR are not competing technologies.**
As I wrote in SPRi's **[2016-014] Virtual Reality/Augmented Reality Technology Development Directions and Implications** report, VR and AR share core technologies. Therefore, from a purely technological perspective, they should be seen as technologies on a continuum rather than competing ones.

{{IMG:2}} [Figure 17] VR/AR Market in Living Spaces (2016-014 - Virtual Reality/Augmented Reality Technology Development Directions and Implications)

However, when simply looking at market size, AR is bound to be larger.

But if you look closely at the reason, AR has the same solutions as the mobile market. Following AR Hardware are aCommerce, AR DATA, AR Voice, and only then do AR content like AR film and TV emerge, with Enterprise AR also appearing as a quite large market. Game and theme park, which received considerable attention in VR, are seen at the very bottom. The AR market is different from VR. Currently, when we show the future of AR, we often present Magic Leap's fantastic games, but the largest actual AR market is already here, touching upon Commerce, AR Voice, and AR Data, which are the biggest markets for AI speakers. It's no coincidence that Uplus released an AR solution connecting AI speakers and TVs.

{{IMG:3}} AR Data, AR Voice already in progress. Neighboring Alexa in the Commerce domain

In short, many platform companies with lifestyle-centric services have moved to secure AR's core technologies, and among them, the devices most closely integrated with users are speakers, TVs, and... smartphones. And AI is used for analyzing data collected in AR. So, you understand why Google is more interested in AR than VR, right? Naver, Kakao, and even Coupang have no choice but to be interested in AR, **because AR is not a competing service with VR, but rather a VR service that has permeated daily life**.

**2) While the scope of AR content needs definition, VR is advantageous for core content.**
So, is all content advantageous for AR? It's accurate to view AR as a child of mobile content. AR technologies can be experienced directly on mobile devices even without AR glasses. In fact, there are many misunderstandings, but AR is not synonymous with cameras or vision. It refers to analyzing and combining real-world data, such as sound and various other information, with virtual world data. Ingress, the predecessor to the representative AR game Pokémon Go, uses only maps and GPS, not a camera. However, you'll know that much content is 'lean-back' (passive content) rather than 'lean-forward'. While TikTok is gaining popularity with short, vertical video content, Disney and Netflix still create cinematic films, lead IPs, and pursue high-quality, horizontal-style content. VR is advantageous for such lean-back, passive content. This is because users don't want ambient noise and information to interfere with their content consumption. In terms of games, if an AR user is an AnyPang user, a VR user is a WoW user. These two markets exist as distinctly different sectors and relatively do not infringe upon each other. And the fact that content has become lighter is due to the device characteristic of mobile's small screen. It's also true that as mobile screens get larger, the proportion of heavy content is increasing. If mobile OTTs like Netflix had launched when the 3-inch iPhone first came out, they would have had to fold. In webtoons and comics, it's now possible to read page-based comics on smartphones with just minor adjustments to the editing format to accommodate mobile. AR inherently has a small FOV (Field of View) and a translucent display for now. It's difficult to focus on high-quality content in such an environment, and it's expected that more active content will emerge as AR combines with VR's native UI. (But many people dislike exercising. Pokémon Go is contributing to human health.) In any case, if the advantage of VR/AR content is solely immersion, it's clear that this effect is diminished in AR. **Look closely at the Digi-Capital data above. Is the AR Game market overwhelmingly larger than the VR Game market? No, it's not.** **Mobile AR, in particular, is a technology far removed from immersion.** **Just read webtoons. Read them twice. You'll be deeply immersed in the story. (You don't necessarily have to read ComicsV.)**

**3) The mobile AR market and the glass AR market should be considered separately.**
As mentioned above, the large market we refer to can also mean the mobile AR market, which is a part of the mobile market. You should not misunderstand this to mean that the glass-type AR market is this large. Below is Digi-Capital's data. Of course, I am also confident that glass-type AR will be a future platform. However, by then, AR glasses will have developed based on significantly advanced VR HMD/glass technology. In reality, AR glasses are currently at a level almost similar to when Oculus first launched in 2014. It was a similar hope for VR, but I hope we don't get too excited or too disappointed too quickly with AR glasses either.

** 3. It's Not AR or VR, What Matters is Immersive Technology Expertise for Each Domain**** **** **In fact, I already included this meaning in a report I wrote in 2016, but it seems there might have been some misunderstanding. Immersive technology in AR/VR is extremely important. Furthermore, as a new platform, there's a high possibility that the industrial landscape could change, just like when the market shifted from PC to mobile. But when the market transitioned from PC to mobile, Kakao rose, but did Naver die? No, it didn't. What's important is how to apply new technologies to each respective domain. As someone who believes that software technology should be internalized as much as possible and assets should be built internally, I think many companies should internalize VR/AR immersive technology, just like software technology. When mobile first emerged, countless outsourcing projects for mobile websites and apps occurred, but most of the companies that truly innovated and succeeded were those that internalized the technology. Nowadays, it seems this sentiment has somewhat taken hold, as it's becoming harder to find computer science graduates in the market.

The same applies to VR/AR. It's not just a technology for making fun games. The user's platform is changing. While wearing a headset can be challenging, each domain needs to consider what can happen within that connected virtual space. While considering interaction is important, the strengths of traditional in-space media and solutions, such as those in architecture and performance, are also very significant. Conversely, those in gaming often have less know-how in these areas or tend to overlook them. Again, in each industry, games and interaction are not the primary focus. It's about considering how the know-how of each industry will transform within that space. And such considerations cannot be solved solely by VR/AR specialized companies. Haven't we already learned that there's no such magic wand in data, AI, or the internet? If you don't have internal assets, you can't effectively use external assets either. If it's an area that hasn't yet taken off, allocating a minimum of internal assets and collaborating with external specialized companies to build expertise and address challenges is also a solution. What's regrettable is the tendency to simply jump on the VR/AR bandwagon without much thought, just because there's a budget for it. I hope to see more genuine companies in VR/AR and more true integration with each specific domain. For reference, ComicsV, which creates VR webtoons, is a company that addresses these challenges within the comic industry.
