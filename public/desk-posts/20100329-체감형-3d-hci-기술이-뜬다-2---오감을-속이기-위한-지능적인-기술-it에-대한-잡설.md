This time, following up on my last post, I'd like to talk a bit about immersive 3D technology, which has recently become a hot topic. From traditional painting to display devices, there hasn't been much significant development in technology that truly makes us feel depth. This is due to the limitations of a single 2D display. However, the recently released movie Avatar introduced people to a new genre of 3D film. Of course, it's not a different concept from what was shown in amusement park 3D experience centers before, but its ability to deliver a very high level of 3D experience throughout the entire film without a sense of incongruity is highly commendable.
****
**1. Fixed 3D Technology Using Binocular Parallax, Suitable for Living Rooms and Cinemas**
First, let me briefly explain the principle behind Avatar and the 3D technology currently being discussed. Its principle lies in creating the binocular image difference, which I explained in the previous post's 3D concept. When viewing a single image, a subtle difference is created between the left and right eyes.
**1) Filming**
To achieve this, two sources for the movie are created from the very beginning. These devices, called stereo cameras, resemble human eyes and can actually be used to measure distance with slight movement.

If the images are created with computer graphics, a single scene is also rendered based on two cameras. The distance between these two cameras is set similar to the distance between human eyes. If there were aliens resembling fish with different distances or angles between their eyes, they wouldn't be able to properly see the 3D movies we watch.
****
**2) Display - Glasses-based Method**
Even if the source is created this way, the most important thing is how to deliver different images to our two eyes. Among these, the most easily accessible method for us is using glasses.
**- Method using polarizing filters**
There are several ways to use glasses, and the method seen when watching Avatar involves projecting two images onto the screen and using polarizing filters in the glasses to separate these two images and deliver them to each eye. You can experience this simply by drawing a picture with red and blue markers and wearing glasses made with red and blue cellophane at home.

This method is not without its problems. First, because a single light is split and projected to both sides, it feels darker compared to regular movies. Also, if you view the screen diagonally from the left or right, the physical visual difference is not reflected in the image difference, leading to a sense of incongruity and eye fatigue.

The video above was created in 2007 by Johnny Chung Lee, who was a postdoctoral researcher at Carnegie Mellon University. The characteristic of this video is that the user's gaze moves. It uses a Wiimote to measure the user's head position and displays a virtual 3D image rendered with that position as the camera's reference. The problem with this method is that if the user doesn't move, they won't perceive it as 3D. However, the advantage of this method is that it can be easily implemented as long as the user's position is known, regardless of whether a Wiimote is used. For example, it's possible with just a camera's face recognition function.
