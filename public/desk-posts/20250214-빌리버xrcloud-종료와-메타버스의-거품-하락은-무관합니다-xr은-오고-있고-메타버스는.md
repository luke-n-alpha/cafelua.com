I'm writing this follow-up post after seeing social media comments linking Believer's shutdown to open-sourcing and the metaverse bubble. It's undeniable that Believer's XRCLOUD received some support amidst the metaverse bubble during the pandemic era. The technology development itself received some help from government projects, and the services introduced probably wouldn't have materialized without that atmosphere. However, while software developer salaries rose significantly, the scale of support received was very small compared to the technology developed. Instead, there was one round of private investment in the middle, and commercialization was delayed while those funds were being exhausted. Furthermore, because we aimed for the highly difficult task of XR devices and the web, it wasn't a structure that could be churned out immediately like a game engine.

A lot of money was spent on the metaverse, and it was hot, but this was linked to cryptocurrencies in the gaming industry due to increased liquidity, and costs were mainly invested in promotional content creation. This made competition difficult for technology-focused companies like ours, whose appearance was less impressive relative to the difficulty, compared to quickly building something with Unreal. Of course, our WebXR-based metaverse was structured to significantly lower content creation costs after technology development, but our mistake was not completing it on time. With the emergence of not only Apple's visionOS but also AndroidXR, XR is now coming to the surface. Both visionOS and AndroidXR have emerged as productivity tools, and WebXR is always separately explained as one pillar of developer tools.

Some people say that XR has failed because Apple's Vision Pro and Meta Quest haven't succeeded like smartphones. I once worked on smart TVs at Naver. I even registered as a developer for early Android TV models and received a Logitech developer kit. Of course, after that, the market was filled with talk of smart TVs failing. "Apple TV is also a failure. IPTV is the answer after all." What about now? In fact, you probably haven't heard news of Android TV and Apple TV being hugely successful. But Uplus's OS is Android TV. All smart TVs except LG and Samsung are Android TVs, and Chromecast and Apple TV still sell well. No one knows it, but it's safe to say they've already established themselves in the TV market. However, due to the nature of TVs, they couldn't threaten the smartphone market, and somewhat unexpectedly, Netflix took the best position in that market. And the talk of tablets being a failure also continued after the iPad came out. Again, I haven't heard that they've been as hugely successful as smartphones, but they've already become mainstream.

So what about XR? The iPhone craze was just an unusual phenomenon that's hard to replicate; markets usually move at that pace. When I graduated with a vision for mobile, it was a time when the mobile bubble theory was in full swing. And large corporations started pulling out their investments, saying it wouldn't work, and the team I was going to join was disbanded. Three years later, the iPhone appeared.

Why don't we consistently invest in technology, and instead want immediate, pretty results? I think the reason the metaverse bubble was excessive is related to that. What about AI? And the government that said it would invest but cut R&D budgets? If there was one factor that had the biggest impact on the discontinuation, it was probably this.

Of course, the biggest reason was my many mistaken choices, failing to create market-appropriate products in a timely manner, failing to prove them with sales, and increasing costs. The metaverse bubble existed, but it didn't. I feel there are similar aspects to AI investment in Korea. There's a lot of talk about the cheaper DeepSeek, but we've never invested as much as DeepSeek. XR is coming. And the metaverse has arrived. Top games are already, still the metaverse. It's just that they weren't the companies we invested in. The stories, images, videos, and 3D created by AI have begun to speak of the world we want to enter.

I send my support to the metaverse developers and CEOs who are still fighting hard with determination. I have vivid memories of moments in this industry. When I touched the keyboard of an 8-bit computer in my elementary school computer lab and the monitor responded, when I spoke broken English to an AI chatbot named Max on Win3.1 and got a reply, I remember thinking of "be able to" to construct the sentence "I'm not good at English." When I played with the Cellvic, a precursor to smartphones, in college, and when I watched a roller coaster VR video with cardboard at the Software Policy Research Institute. All of these are fundamental experiences, and what software aims for. At the time, when I shared such experiences, everyone told me not to make a fuss. The reaction was, "Is that even possible???" But there's no way it wouldn't be possible, right? I just couldn't do it. And I gave up on the business, not on development. I'm still here, and I'll open the code today.

I just have a lot of regrets about the business world that fluctuates too quickly, and about superficial companies. In college, "A website? That'll take a week, right?" (Nowadays, with AI, it can be done in a day.. haha, but the pressure seems to remain.)
