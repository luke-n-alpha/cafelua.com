Yesterday, I met a high school friend. He's from SI development and is preparing for a job change, so we talked, and I felt very frustrated and sorry for him. Though we were in the same SW development field, there were different processes, different cultures, and a sense of defeat that things couldn't be changed. This is a general feeling I get about developers in the SI sector. I talked at length about the culture of internet companies. My friend then said it felt like he had been hit on the head. I truly felt sorry.

The development culture of internet companies didn't just fall from the sky. They are simply companies that practice what is inherent in the most basic properties of software and what was taught in computer science at university. Debugging and fixing based on logs or output, merging repetitive code through refactoring, and improving performance with code if the order can be changed or processed upfront. Since debugging is difficult if you build and test all at once, they write code little by little, verifying small units with iterative testing, and coding remote APIs based on their references; this entire process is simply embedded throughout the company.

However, because SI receives money on an hourly basis, repetitive code is preferred as it increases time, and despite the ability to improve performance through process improvements, it's important to cater to the client, so no harsh words are spoken. Ultimately, delivering results and getting paid is key, so not quick feedback but only the acceptance point matters. Design modifications are allowed for continuous business relationships, leading to broken software, angry clients, ruined developer lives, damaged profits for the contracting company, and a lack of mutual trust requiring constant oversight.

The biggest problem with SI is that by not adhering to the principles learned in school, the software, like a child, gets broken, and trust is shattered, giving the impression that the industry itself is broken.

And because Korean developers are 'nice,' they often say, 'Shouldn't we still finish what we've been assigned?' I asked them, why, when software is so important, can't they say NO, and why does it ultimately become their development responsibility again? If you have no authority but only responsibility, why are you there? If you feel nothing is being internalized within yourself and you're not growing, why are you part of that organization? I said. And if you ask if that company guarantees tenure like a civil servant? They say no.

However, I believe calling a developer who does planning, development, and operations a full-stack developer is a significant misunderstanding. It's something that should have clearly been met with a NO.

A full-stack developer is a developer capable of building a service that can run independently. In the case of startups, speed is crucial, so working software is needed first. At the same time, they must be able to respond quickly in the long term. Speed ultimately means sacrificing some perfection, knowing when that perfection should be achieved, and being able to deploy people or identify suitable technologies and solutions. I believe such a developer is a full-stack developer.

And if you ask who a full-stack developer is, again, we learned it all in school. We learned everything from OS to web, so why aren't we full-stack developers? What's important is being able to do it. Even a developer with excellent full-stack skills is difficult to work with if they negatively impact those around them. Ultimately, for developers in the SI sector, what's needed more than skill is a positive attitude. It's truly regrettable that they possess the skill to build something, yet they talk about why it won't work.

The ability required of a software developer is to make things happen. And they should talk as if it can be done. What cannot be done must be stated clearly as impossible. Instead of always working for 10 and getting paid 10, they should always work for 10, then develop something that can do 10 in 1, and ask for 8. This should be something the client hadn't even thought of.

SI companies have 2nd and 3rd place players in specific areas. However, in the essence of reusable software, there's no reason for 2nd or 3rd place to exist. As important as software is, there's no reason to use 2nd or 3rd place products cheaply. On the contrary, the 1st place product has more reasons to be cheaper. Therefore, a characteristic of internet companies is that there are no 2nd or 3rd place players. Each company may seem to be in the same industry, but they are 1st place companies that have survived by targeting different users. The idea of making something 'good enough' to compete on price and getting paid for the work you put in seems quite widespread in the SI sector, but this is not 'software-like'.

Making software 'good enough' and selling it cheaply. Pricing it based on input time and lines of code... Is this truly suitable for software?

Even as the software industry grows and becomes the future, the desire for customization will not disappear. However, what's certain is that customization is an inherent property of software: using open source to tailor it to one's own taste, and weaving together various external sources. Paying for what you use and paying for the value experienced is the most suitable approach for software. And the evaluation of software developers should also be based on the value of the code they create. If the software I create is the same as what someone else created, why create it? How is that different from coding by looking at a textbook in school? Computers are better at copying than humans.

The important thing is that we've already learned it all through software. It's time to put it into practice. You have the ability to create, so why can't you prove your value like a professional? The ability is already there. What's needed is confidence, and doing things as you've learned. If you give up, nothing will change.

**If what you've learned is consistently ignored, that place is dangerous. Escape.**
It means that a company that follows the right principles can create better software and dominate the market at any time.
