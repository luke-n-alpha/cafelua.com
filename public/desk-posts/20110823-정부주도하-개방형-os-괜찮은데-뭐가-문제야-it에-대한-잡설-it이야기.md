정부 주도하에 한국형 OS를 만든다는 소식에 명박이가 할일은 임기나 제대로 마치는거라며 비아냥거리는 이야기와 정부 주도로 언제 제대로 한 적이 있느냐고 실패를 예측하는 이들이 많은것 같습니다. 저도 현정부의 IT정책에 비판적이었던 블로거였던 만큼 같은 뜻일거라고 생각하실지 모르겠으나 사실 전 정부의 의견에 찬성합니다. 그 이야기를 해볼까 합니다.
**1. 국가 추진하면 다 안된다 ? 그럼 정통부 부활론은 잘못된건가 ?**일단 MB취임직후 IT정책에 가장 큰 수술을 한 것은 정통부 폐지입니다. 정통부가 그동안 일을 잘해왔는가는 일단 젖혀두고라도 IT산업에 힘을 빼려한 정부의 의도는 분명합니다. 방통위와 산자부 문화부 교육부에서 통신, 인터넷, 게임 등등을 흩어서 가져갔고, 그동안 해온일이라고는 몇가지 규제 말고는 사실 그다지 한건 없는 것 같습니다.  그리고 이와 동시에 실종된것은 한국 IT업계에 대한 고민입니다. 한국의 폐쇄 OS라고 대중들에게 잘못알려진 wipi역시 정통부 작품입니다. 이전 포스팅에서 이야기를 했지만 당시에는 그만한 고민이 있었기 때문에 그만한 작품이 나온겁니다. ax기반의 공인인증서와 결제시스템도 당시에는 납득할만한 기술이었습니다. 아니 오히려 전세계에서 가장 앞서나갔던것도 사실입니다. 그러나 문제는 그 기술 자체가 아니라 시대가 변했음에도 불구하고 현정부는 이전정부의 기술을 고수한다는것입니다. 때문에 wipi와 ax를 정통부의 실책으로 보는데는 반대하는 입장입니다. 물론 그 기술이 지금까지 유효하다는 이야기도 경쟁력이 있다는 이야기도 아닙니다. 문제는 제대로 시작한 변화라고 해도 시대에 맞게 기술을바꿔줘야 하는것이죠. 그것을 어쩌면 정통부 막판에 잘 하지 못했고, 주도처도 없는 현정부는 그냥 손만 놓고 있는 느낌입니다.따라서 정부가 하는 일이 모두 실패라는 말은 옳지 않다고 봅니다. 오히려 전세계에서 가장 큰 소프트웨어 프로젝트와 체계화된 소프트웨어 생산기술을 가진곳이 NASA라는 점도 국가기반 프로젝트가 실패한다는것은 선입견이라고 봅니다. 더군다나 한국의 IT역사는 그리 길지도 않습니다.

Upon hearing the news that a Korean OS will be created under government leadership, many are making sarcastic remarks, saying that all MB needs to do is properly finish his term, and predicting failure, asking when a government-led initiative has ever succeeded. As a blogger who was critical of the current government's IT policies, you might think I'd share the same sentiment, but in fact, I agree with the government's opinion. I'd like to talk about that.

**1. If the state pushes it, does it all fail? Then is the argument for reviving the Ministry of Information and Communication wrong?**
First, the biggest change to IT policy immediately after MB took office was the abolition of the Ministry of Information and Communication (MIC). Setting aside whether the MIC had been doing a good job, the government's intention to weaken the IT industry was clear. The Korea Communications Commission (KCC), Ministry of Knowledge Economy, Ministry of Culture, and Ministry of Education scattered and took over responsibilities for telecommunications, internet, games, etc., and it seems they haven't done much beyond a few regulations. At the same time, concern for the Korean IT industry disappeared. WiPi, which was mistakenly known to the public as Korea's closed OS, was also a product of the MIC. As I mentioned in a previous post, there was a significant amount of thought behind it at the time, which led to such a creation. AX-based public certificates and payment systems were also understandable technologies at the time. In fact, it's true that they were even ahead of the rest of the world. However, the problem isn't the technology itself, but that the current government adheres to the previous government's technology despite changing times. Therefore, I disagree with viewing WiPi and AX as MIC's failures. Of course, I'm not saying that these technologies are still valid or competitive. The problem is that even a properly initiated change needs its technology updated to fit the times. Perhaps the MIC failed to do this well in its final stages, and the current government, lacking a leading entity, seems to be merely standing by.
Therefore, I believe it's incorrect to say that everything the government does fails. On the contrary, the fact that NASA has the world's largest software projects and systematized software production technology suggests that the idea of state-based projects failing is a prejudice. Furthermore, Korea's IT history is not that long.

**2. Who will create an open-source OS?**
Returning to the OS discussion, who can create an open-source OS? How much core technology has the Korean software industry accumulated so far? Have there been any successful languages or OSes created in Korea? If you try to do such things in Korea, you're likely to starve. Most software jobs have been concentrated in application software. There are some system software positions at the lower level, thanks to the electronics sector. However, pure computer science as an academic discipline is virtually extinct. People say it's something for schools to do. But even schools are busy with industry-academia application software development for research achievements. Computer science graduate programs, even at prestigious universities, are under-enrolled. When I consulted a well-known professor about graduate school, he even advised me not to go to domestic graduate schools. Why is it that Korea doesn't have an MIT, and why are there no pure computer science experts even if we wanted to develop an OS? There are many Java developers, but few VM developers. Because it's an area that requires a lot of money and cannot quickly gain competitiveness compared to overseas, companies avoid it, and since that's the type of talent companies want, schools also produce such talent. In short, it's unknown how many people in Korea can currently create an open-source OS. Therefore, even if we start now, success is uncertain. But if we don't start now, we'll never be able to create one, and we'll always have to pay high license fees overseas for software production.
Government-led, non-profit technology acquisition projects provide an environment for these high-level talents to conduct research. Do we ponder why there are no Bill Gates or Steves Jobs in Korea? The answer is simple. Software is not popular in Korea, and developers struggle to make a living. This becomes even more severe for high-level technicians. It's even said that a developer's highest skill is politics.

**3. I welcome the development of a domestic open-source OS. (But properly, not just throwing money away or for show...)**
I welcome the development of a domestic open-source OS. **However, I oppose short-term, results-oriented development, I oppose the state using this as a technology transfer fee to conduct G2B (government-to-business) projects, and I oppose enforcing this too strongly.**
The advantage of an open-source OS is its openness. However, as a private American company currently leads this open-source OS, there's a question of how long it will maintain a neutral stance. (In fact, it's not completely neutral even now.) A government-led, or rather, government-invested, open-source OS project is a very good idea. What's important going forward is to pursue it properly with determination.
Practical, S-class talent isn't only needed by Samsung Electronics. We need practical-minded leaders, not just those with bureaucratic careers.
If they show the same level of commitment as the Four Major Rivers Project, it will surely succeed. (Though the possibility seems low.) I hope this isn't just a show, like the fish robot or Myungtendo, where a tiny bit of money is thrown around to say, "We're interested in IT too." (Proper game development costs 10 billion won.) **Frankly, it's a fact that it lacks competitiveness compared to Android or iOS. Even with a lot of money, results won't come quickly. And it can't catch up in a short time, because it wasn't built in a short time. Also, if the plan is to exploit engineers and burn them out, please give up. What's important is securing a minimum level of technology and talent, and using that as nourishment for the IT industry. In that regard, OS development, which must be supported by computer science technology, is a welcome endeavor.**
While the government is untrustworthy, seeing the overwhelming pessimism even before a proper direction has begun, I wonder what the difference is from saying, "We can't be like scary North Korea; America must protect us." (This might even be the public opinion the reluctant government wants...) What's important now is not the project's success, but its first step. The government, which completely overturned the well-progressing IT policies of the Roh Moo-hyun administration like a daughter-in-law's meal table just because the administration changed, is suddenly changing its stance. Although it's untrustworthy, I hope they look to the long term and start running again.
