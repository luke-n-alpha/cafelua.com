A survey announced that apps won the battle between web and apps. Regarding this news, someone even left a joking comment, "When did they ever fight?" I also cast my vote in favor of this joke. In fact, these two have never fought. Their technological positions are also different. However, for those looking to start a new mobile project, choosing between these two solutions can feel like a confrontation. To clarify this confusion, I'd like to explain the platform landscape that has emerged in apps and the web.

**1. Why Apps Won in the App vs. Web Battle**
Why did apps win in the app vs. web battle?

**1) Dominance of a Few Mobile OSes**
In fact, the objectives of these two technologies are quite different. **Web, which operates based on browsers and standards, boasts multi-platform support and real-time deployability, while apps, based on device or platform vendor APIs, ensure fast and optimized performance on the platform.** The reason for apps' victory lies in environmental changes regarding which technology better suits its purpose. It's precisely the dominance of Android OS. If multiple platform vendors dominate the market, third parties inevitably have to increase their reliance on web technologies to support various platforms. The reason why subsequent OSes like Tizen and Firefox adopted web technologies is precisely due to these characteristics of web technology. This allows them to claim the advantage of covering existing major OSes with a single app development.

**2) Browsers Disappear, Web Combines with Mobile Platforms**
While using Galaxy and iPhone, users often don't realize that the 'Internet' icon refers to Chrome and Safari. Furthermore, platform vendors intentionally place apps front and center. This is partly because apps can handle most of a smartphone's native functionalities. Searches are conducted in the iTunes and Google Play markets, and users find, install, arrange, and then run individual apps. Searches on Naver or Google are also performed through the Naver or Google apps, not through a browser. However, the web has not disappeared. Instead, the functionalities that the web can perform are evolving by being integrated into apps. Representative apps like Naver, Google, or Facebook apps are adopting web technologies. By embedding a browser within the app itself and displaying web pages inside the app, development costs are reduced. If the web vs. app battle were viewed as a traffic battle between sockets and HTTP, the outcome might have been different. Therefore, the need for web technologies remains very high, and it is crucial to appropriately adopt web technologies even during app development.

**2. The Web is Undergoing a Major Shift**
**1) Misconceptions about the Web: The Web also experiences fragmentation.**
When describing web technology, it's often said that a single source can perfectly run on multiple platforms. However, my professor's words from my school days, "A generic solution does nothing perfectly," are absolutely true. While generic, it won't be very smooth unless customized for individual platforms. The reason for this is that while web technology has standards, the browsers that adhere to them do not have a single standard. If there were universally recognized international laws, it would be easier to understand by imagining that each country uses and interprets them slightly differently. Typically, if you only develop for Internet Explorer, you'll encounter issues where it doesn't function correctly or the layout breaks in Chrome or Netscape. Such cases were relatively rare on mobile because Safari on Android and iOS, and Google's browser, were all using the same rendering engine, "WebKit." Of course, while the rendering engine might be the same, other factors also influence browser performance. From the parser that interprets and analyzes HTML, to the DOM tree, and the JavaScript engine that runs JavaScript. Although web technology is broadly referred to as HTML5, its actual implementation involves a much wider variety of components developed by different companies. Even a single OS or browser has minor issues with each version, so it's difficult to expect that products developed by different companies, even if they adhere to standards, will function perfectly identically. Therefore, in web development, the process usually involves developing based on a major platform or a browser that causes relatively fewer problems in other browsers, and then fixing issues in other browsers. Given this environment, it's not surprising that developers have a high preference for the "Chrome" browser.

**2) Major Shift in the WebKit-Dominated Mobile Browser Market**
However, the WebKit-dominated mobile browser market, which iPhone ignited and Chrome solidified, is showing signs of change. As mentioned above, WebKit refers to a rendering engine, and a change in rendering engines is a primary cause of fragmentation for developers. Until now, Internet Explorer used its own rendering engine developed by Microsoft. The existing rendering engine landscape was such that Chrome and Safari, using WebKit, dominated most of the mobile market, while the smaller PC market included Mozilla Firefox's Gecko engine (a representative open-source group), and Opera's and IE's proprietary rendering engines. In fact, it was no exaggeration to say WebKit had unified the world. This was also a reason why rapid growth in mobile web was possible. However, according to recent news, due to conflicts with Apple, which operates WebKit, Google has declared independence with an engine called Blink.

**- Google Declares Independence with Blink**
First, the largest chunk of WebKit has declared independence. And Opera's subtle touch (recently abandoned its own engine, briefly went to WebKit, and eventually to Google...)

**- Firefox Isn't Dead Yet. Gather with Gecko**
And Firefox's Gecko engine might gain support from MS and Samsung. (Tizen is also known to use the WebKit engine. It's highly likely to change to Gecko in the future.)

**Consequently, the WebKit-based mobile web market, once led by Apple, seems poised to transform into a landscape of WebKit (Apple) vs. Blink (Google/Opera) vs. Gecko (Firefox/Samsung/MS).**

**3. The Fierce Power Struggle for Mobile Web Dominance is Due to Great Opportunities**
This is a fierce power struggle among players for dominance in the mobile web. **Developers might lose further interest in the web due to concerns about fragmentation during this process. However, what is clear is that this power struggle for dominance signifies that these players are well aware of the sweet fruits available in the mobile web.** My personal prediction is that web technologies like JavaScript will also be universally adopted, much like the C language provides generic support for all platforms. Successfully running these will be the platform's capability. Therefore, there's no need to simply view the market as app vs. web. The important question is: what resources and technologies should be used to provide the best experience for the users of the project being developed? The web vs. app dynamic is a familiar one, seen previously in Java vs. C, and Windows vs. Linux. However, if we look at it from a software development perspective, rather than just a coding level, I believe these two technologies don't need to be in opposition. The web and apps will not end with a complete victory for one side. What's important is where our customers are.
