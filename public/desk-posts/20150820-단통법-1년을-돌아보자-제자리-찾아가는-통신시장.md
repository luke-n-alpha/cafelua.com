**1. 단통법 효과 누가 믿을만한 이야기를 하는가?** 2014년 10월 단말기 유통구조 개선법이 실시됬다. 그 이후 모든 국민들이 폰을 다 비싸게 사게되는 네티즌들의 의견이 있었고, 그 이후 기사들을 검색해보면 다소 상반된 의견들이 상당히 주를 이룬다. 일단 개인적으로 네티즌의 의견은 무시할만하다고 본다.   단통법 이전에 싸게사는 방법들을 자주 이용했던 경험에 의하면, 당시 싸게 살수 있는 사람들은 극히 일부에 불과하다. 주말에 뜨는 보조금, 야간에 스팟으로 뜨는 버스를 탔던 경험에 의하면 이를 수행할 수 있는 이들은 많지 않았다. 인터넷에 익숙한 아주 소수에게만 큰 혜택이 돌아가는 구조였다. 사실 고백하자면 그런 혜택을 나는 잘 이용했고 주위의 사람들에게도 도움을 줬다.  하지만, 내가 일반적인 사용자가 아님을 인터넷 서비스를 했던 입장에서 나는 아주 잘 안다. 또한 소수의 사용자라고 하더라도 그 박탈감이 심하면 매우 시끄러워지는데, 단통법의 실시는 시끄러운 소수의 네티즌들의 이익을 뺏는 구조였기 때문에 네티즌의 의견은 신뢰하기 어렵다. 그러면 언론을 신뢰할 수 있을까? 이 역시 쉽지 않다고 본다. 언론의 가장 큰 광고주는 이동통신사와 제조사이다. 정부보다 눈치를 봐야 하는게 광고주인 만큼 어느쪽 장단에 춤을 추느냐에 따라 매우 다른 논조의 의견을 내기 마련이다.  따라서 믿을만한것은 객관적인 수치와 주변에서 실질적으로 피부로 와닿는 내용 밖에 없다고 생각한다.​**2. 나는 기본적으로 단통법의 도입취지에 찬성** 먼저, 나는 단통법의 도입의 취지에 찬성한다. 다소 사회주의적인 과도한 개입처럼 보이지만, 3개밖에 안되는 대기업에 더군다나 대체선택 수단도 없고, 그동안 담합의 증거가 매우 오래전부터 여러번 발견되어 징계까지 다수 받은 이동통신사의 시장은 개입이 필요하다. 또한 과도한 마켓팅과 한집 건너 하나 있는 단말기 유통점도 뭔가 비현실적이다. 2년에 한번 바꾸는 스마트폰이 중국집 보다 많다는건 뭔가 이상한 일이 아닌가? 따라서 분명 소비자는 과도한 지출을 하는데 일조하고 있다. 영세상인들을 살리는 것이 목적이라면 차라리 통닭을 한번 더 시켜먹겠다.​

**1. Effects of the Terminal Distribution Act: Who is telling a trustworthy story?**
The Terminal Distribution Act (단통법) was implemented in October 2014. Since then, there have been opinions from netizens that all citizens are now forced to buy phones at higher prices, and subsequent news articles show quite a few conflicting opinions. Personally, I tend to disregard the opinions of netizens. Based on my experience of frequently using methods to buy phones cheaply before the Terminal Distribution Act, only a very small fraction of people were able to buy them at low prices back then. From my experience of catching subsidies that appeared on weekends or "buses" (spot deals) that popped up at night, not many people could take advantage of them. It was a structure where significant benefits went only to a very small number of people who were familiar with the internet. To be honest, I made good use of such benefits and helped people around me as well.

However, having worked in internet services, I know very well that I am not a typical user. Moreover, even if it's a minority of users, if their sense of deprivation is severe, things can get very noisy. Since the implementation of the Terminal Distribution Act took away the benefits of a noisy minority of netizens, their opinions are difficult to trust. Then, can we trust the media? I don't think that's easy either. The biggest advertisers for the media are mobile carriers and manufacturers. As advertisers are entities they must be more mindful of than the government, they tend to express opinions with very different tones depending on whose tune they are dancing to.

Therefore, I believe that only objective figures and information that is tangibly felt by people around us can be trusted.

**2. I fundamentally agree with the purpose of the Terminal Distribution Act's introduction.**
First, I agree with the purpose of introducing the Terminal Distribution Act. Although it may seem like an excessive, somewhat socialist intervention, intervention is necessary in a market dominated by only three large corporations, where there are no alternative choices, and where evidence of collusion has been found multiple times over a long period, leading to numerous disciplinary actions against mobile carriers. Furthermore, excessive marketing and device distribution stores, which seem to be on every other block, are somewhat unrealistic. Isn't it strange that there are more smartphone stores than Chinese restaurants for a device we replace every two years? Therefore, consumers are clearly contributing to excessive spending. If the goal is to save small business owners, I'd rather order fried chicken one more time.

**3. Let's re-examine only the trustworthy information.**
In January 2015, mobile carriers' ARPU (Average Revenue Per User) surged, and a quick search of articles on this attributes the cause to decreased marketing costs and an increase in high-paying subscribers. In other words, the goal of reducing marketing costs, which was the intent of the Terminal Distribution Act, was achieved, but it failed to lighten the burden on the public's wallets. Recent ARPU articles show a decrease in ARPU in May 2015, and some articles suggest this is due to the effects of the Terminal Distribution Act. Whether it's a success or a failure, there are a few things that everyone generally accepts as truth.

**1) Mobile carriers' marketing costs have decreased.**
First, a decrease in marketing costs. Marketing costs are money. Creating advertisements costs a surprisingly large amount of money, and executing them also incurs significant expenses. And the source of that money is the consumer.

**2) Low-cost phones have become more active.**
Second, the activation of low-cost phones. The Terminal Distribution Act is cited as the reason for this, and there are claims that Pantech and LG faced difficulties because of it. If one attributes the difficulties of Pantech and LG to the Terminal Distribution Act, based on my past experience in making mobile phones, I think it's an absurd claim. This is because both Pantech and LG are companies with very high overseas sales ratios. Whether low-cost or high-cost, they could have made a profit by creating well-made products with a technological gap. However, customer value has shifted from hardware to software, and their struggle against the high quality of low-cost Chinese products is not unique to the Korean market. Apple's success, on the other hand, is due to China not being able to produce iOS phones, allowing Apple to avoid direct competition and maintain unique differentiation. However, Korean companies developing Android phones directly compete with China, and with the standardization of hardware, they have to compete on price, which inevitably makes the fight with China very difficult. For similar reasons, even the number one company, Samsung, is not in a great situation. Even with the very well-made Galaxy Edge series, it's difficult to achieve the glory of the past with hardware differentiation alone.

**3) Rate plans have become predictable and simpler.**
Third, predictable and simplified data-centric rate plans. Before, you had to wait and agonize over when to buy a smartphone and search for information, but now you can buy one at a fair price anytime. This means that instead of timing and guesswork, you can now focus on the actual product and the mobile carrier's service when making a choice. There's a reason a bottle of soju is made to yield seven and a half glasses; rate plans were designed to be this intricate and complex, and the more complex they are, the more disadvantageous they are to the consumer. When factors like subsidies are added, the complexity increases even further. Moreover, for seniors who only make voice calls and use KakaoTalk, when replacing their phones, they no longer need to pay for 6GB or more of data that they won't even use for three months. They can now switch to a very affordable plan with enough data for KakaoTalk and internet use without much trouble. Looking at the expenditure over three months, it was actually over 200,000 won, which was comparable to the price of a cheap device these days. I actually replaced my parents' smartphones through an MVNO, and both received FHD quad-core smartphones with plans in the early 30,000 won range. Through this, they enjoy video calls and leave comments on their grandchildren's photos on KakaoStory.

Are you dissatisfied because you can't buy the latest phones cheaply due to the Terminal Distribution Act? I want to ask if you truly need that phone. The younger generation is called the "880,000 won generation." What the 880,000 won generation absolutely needs to pay for is communication fees. Regardless of whether it's expensive or cheap, mobile communication is now like electricity and water. Should we raise everyone's communication fees and cause hardship to these people and to parents who only make voice calls, just so a few can buy expensive phones cheaply? Are those low-cost phones still so bad that you can't even run games on them like before? On the contrary, I'm truly glad that data communication fees have become more realistic. When the iPhone 3GS first came out, the "i-plan" was the first usable data plan. Since then, as LTE got faster, rate plans also skyrocketed, but after the Terminal Distribution Act, data fees decreased and stabilized. Now that the Terminal Distribution Act has stabilized, I imagine those who are unhappy are the manufacturers and mobile carriers who forcibly sold expensive smartphones with marketing costs, the advertising market that created their ads, and perhaps mobile carrier sales outlets. There were certainly too many of them for too long.

**After the Terminal Distribution Act, things are simply returning to their proper place. We were enchanted by the magic of the iPhone, but now it's time to return to reality. Jobs is dead, and the smartphone party is over. Now, the opponents we must fight are Tim Cook and Xiaomi.**
