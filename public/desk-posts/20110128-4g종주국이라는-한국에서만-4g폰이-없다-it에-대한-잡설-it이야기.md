**1. 망이 아직 준비되지 않았기 때문에 출시하지 않는다 ?**

Wifi를 통한 무료인터넷 대신 2g를 통한 이용만 장려하려 했던 것이죠. 이는 한국 스마트폰을 보급하는데 가장 큰 걸림돌이 되었고, 아이폰3gs 보급이후 당연하다는듯이 이제 wifi를 탑재하고 나오고 있고, 요즘은 피쳐폰도 wifi를 달고 나옵니다.

Why is Korea, supposedly the home of 4G, so late? 

**1. Not launching because the network isn't ready yet?**
 This is partly true. In the case of LTE, it's genuinely not ready, and for WiBro, it's not yet a nationwide network. However, technology already exists for 4G phones to support 3G, 2G, and even Wi-Fi, just as 3G phones support 2G. In fact, KT once released such a smartphone based on Windows Mobile.
It's false to say that WiBro isn't ready when Wi-Fi is being relayed on moving subways using WiBro. After all, there's not much difference since 3G internet isn't smooth in rural areas anyway. 

**2. Is WiBro experiencing the same sorrow that Wi-Fi smartphones once did?**
A major complaint from domestic users when early smartphones were released was that only Korean smartphones came without Wi-Fi. I recently laughed when I heard that iPhones in China would exclude Wi-Fi, but that was actually the story in Korea until recently. Although officially never admitted by carriers at the time, the reason is clear with a little thought: they wanted to encourage 2G usage instead of free internet via Wi-Fi. This became the biggest obstacle to the spread of smartphones in Korea. After the iPhone 3GS became popular, Wi-Fi became standard, and these days, even feature phones come with Wi-Fi.

Currently, there are compatibility issues with overseas networks due to the frequency difference between WiMAX and WiBro, but it has already been decided to change this. If a phone can use 3G alongside, simply using WiBro for data communication alone holds significant meaning. 

**4. What mobile carriers truly fear is the deterioration of profitability due to mVoIP**
Blocking Wi-Fi was believed to infringe upon wireless internet revenue, but it seems they believe WiBro could lead to a more direct deterioration of profitability. And to some extent, this is a reality. Even without WiBro, unlimited internet plans and increasingly cheaper data plans contribute to this. In the case of LG U+, 1GB is standard, which translates to about 800 minutes of voice chat. (For high-quality voice calls, it's 20kbps per second, 1.2MB per minute, 72MB per hour). **However, with WiBro, 30GB for 20,000 won allows for 400 hours of voice chat. It can be considered virtually unlimited.**
 

 {{IMG:2}} 
 
 Voice chat, once on MSN, is gradually being adopted by all mobile SNS platforms and is becoming an essential app for smartphone subscribers. Smartphone users already tend to use KakaoTalk more than SMS. KakaoTalk has not only set its direction towards supporting voice chat, but domestic portal Daum and global company Google will also support it. Google, as the creator of the Android OS, means that almost all Android users have a Gmail account and will likely be able to make calls using it. 

**5. The reason these developments began: Separation of services and infrastructure**
**And the greater possibilities that arise from it**
The direct cause of these developments is that general-purpose OSs like iOS and Android are independent of infrastructure. Traditional wireless internet was no different from the absurd situation where using Powercom meant being forced to use Daum as the homepage and having to create a Daum ID. However, the smartphone market has ushered in an era where users can freely change their homepage, even create web servers, and anyone can start a web service. This market, which mobile carriers fear, actually creates tremendous new opportunities. 

**6. Mobile carriers in a dilemma: Time to find more realistic and future-oriented alternatives**
This market is likely a path mobile carriers do not want. However, now that the paradigm has shifted to smartphones, there's no way to block various services, and most importantly, the explosively increasing data traffic cannot be handled by existing 3G or Wi-Fi models. Providing users with technology that increases available data traffic cheaply would obviously lead to a decline in profitability. If 50GB of WiBro is combined with voice calls, the current flagship smartphone plan, the 55, effectively loses its power. Even if we deduct a device promotion value of 20,000 won, it would be around 35,000 won, which only makes it comparable to WiBro's pricing. 

**- Is LTE deployment the best option for carriers?**
 Perhaps pricing LTE at the same level as 3G would be the best option. And with all the noise about LTE these days, I'm worried they might be heading in that direction. The problem is that Korean carriers, having been late in smartphone adoption, have not been proactive in building 4G infrastructure like LTE, and now that they have to start, it's difficult timing-wise.
 **Ultimately, during such a build-out process, the stagnation of Korea's smartphone service market will be unavoidable, and overseas countries that have already launched 4G services will once again be two steps ahead. Even developing countries, which are incomparable to us, are building WiMAX. Are we really the home of 4G?**

 To take a step closer to a seamless world, we must consider what our best choice is. Should we sacrifice the potential of the entire Korean IT market, which can expand globally, for the temporary profit preservation of a few domestic mobile carriers? Furthermore, mobile carriers also need to intensely deliberate on their position in this future landscape and what they can do in the expanding wireless infrastructure market.
