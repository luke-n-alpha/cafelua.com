A few days ago, I attended a regular PAG discussion forum. The topic was about IoT, which is currently heating up with Samsung's Galaxy Gear and Google's Android Wear. Overall, the prevailing opinion was that it's still in a stage similar to PDAs before the iPhone came out, where each blind person is touching a different part of the elephant. It was also impressive to see how different companies approach IoT based on their perspectives. Since it was a free-form discussion among participants, and the concrete forms of IoT were still unclear, there wasn't much to summarize, but the following points stuck with me.

**----- Discussion Summary -----**

**0. Date: April 9, 2014 (Wed) 7 PM ~ 9 PM**
- Topic: The Potential of Android Wear

**1. IoT is still in its early stages**
- Sales are not as high as expected
- Expensive price relative to value
- There is no perfect connection technology for IoT yet, so numerous connections are being experimented with.
* In PCs, cables, and in smartphones, the emergence of 3G and LTE brought about connection technologies perfectly suited for smartphones, which greatly grew the smartphone market.
* A concept like "all IP" might not be necessary, but Wi-Fi and Bluetooth don't seem suitable.
- B2B is relatively clear, but killer services for B2C are still difficult to confirm.

**2. Classification of Perspectives on IoT (3 types)**
* These three elements are not necessarily clearly distinct, but they can be analyzed from three perspectives.
1) A means to solidify existing businesses: Customer touchpoints for online businesses to expand offline (Amazon Dash)
2) Device sales businesses
2.1) Purpose is device sales itself: Service price included in the device price (Nike FuelBand)
2.2) Purpose is to become a platform: Inducing 3rd Party participation (Sparrow, Dice+)

**3. Is IoT a horizontal or vertical platform?**
Looking at the history of platforms, platforms initially entered as vertical platforms and tended to expand into horizontal platforms as their volume grew. Will it go that way again this time?

**3.1. Vertical Platform**
2.1.1. Health 2.0
- Samsung's IoT platform
- For platforms in specific fields, the barrier to entry for developers is high, making it difficult to build scale in terms of 3rd Party pools.

**3.2. Horizontal Platform**
3.2.1. Google/Apple's IoT
- A clear intention to merge all data from connectivity devices around a cloud account and drive them.

**4. IoT is still more effective for B2B than B2C**
- Clear purpose despite imperfect technology
- From an investor's perspective, B2B is clearer and easier to approach.

**5. Smart tools (smart things) are becoming finely differentiated**
- Vertical elements tend to become more segmented.
5.1. Differentiated tool: Smartwatch: The wrist is the easiest place to access by modifying something already worn.

I also briefly tried Google Glass myself, and while it was a fun gadget for developers to play with, as a consumer, I didn't really warm up to it. I didn't want to pay $1500 for a VGA-quality camera just to upload photos to KakaoStory in real-time. To put Google Glass simply, it was a $1500 Facebook + Chrome browser + messenger. Most things were inconvenient and unfamiliar, except for the freedom of both hands. As a Korean, my English wasn't good enough, so voice recognition was an issue, but above all, controlling the glasses with finger touches and swipes was truly inconvenient. It might have been the best interface in the limited space of glasses, but it rendered the "hands-free" aspect meaningless.

In the case of Google Moto, the impression of Google NOW on the first screen is that Google services are front and center, rather than 3rd Party apps that have been embedded in smart devices until now, giving a strong feeling that it's a Google device for Google. I'm not sure if it's because it still feels like a vertical stage, as mentioned above, but what's clear is that Google Now is at the heart of Google's IoT. I tried to use Google NOW on my Galaxy S3, but the information Google Now provided in Korea was extremely limited, making it not very useful. Furthermore, as someone who mostly uses Naver's calendar and mail instead of Google's default calendar and email, the utility of these Google services is even lower for me.

Currently, everyone is like the blind men touching an elephant when it comes to the direction IoT will take, but I do think there are a few services that could work well on smartwatches. Let me discuss a few.

*****
Services I think could work well on smartwatches

**1) Theme Services**
The reason Galaxy Fit and Google Moto received enthusiastic responses from users was because they were pretty. The fundamental value of a watch has a very strong element of being seen by others. For smartwatches to spread, users need to feel "cool" when using them. And, starting this year, there's a trend of such perception spreading, which makes me quite optimistic. Extending this thought, the best services will inevitably be those that make the watch look pretty, i.e., theme and skin services. Especially for smartwatches, where the screen occupies most of the space, users will be able to change skins daily for a new feeling, which will certainly look cool.
- Weather and clock will be the best fit for these theme services.

**2) PIMS services with high alarm needs**
Looking at Google Android Wear's API, it feels like they're obsessed with alarms. This makes sense, as Google's services strongly feel like they're assuming a smartphone. Even in PDAs, traditional services like calendar, mail, and address book (PIMS) have the highest need for alarms. Additionally, for smartphones, the desire for messenger, SMS, and calls will be highest. In fact, one of the advantages of smartwatches was introduced as "you won't miss incoming calls."

**3) Precise location-based, definitive search beyond GPS**
Google NOW is the most representative example. It's an agent-type search that automatically shows what you're most curious about or what's most worth seeing at the moment. It has high technical difficulty and requires sufficient content. Furthermore, it demands much more accurate location, including indoors, than GPS. Like necessary information when you enter a specific store.

**4) Payment, Authentication, Smart Wallet**
Attempts at smart wallets are currently being made with smartphones. However, smartphones are not necessarily more convenient to carry than traditional wallets. It's just that both wallets and smartphones are essential items, so the idea was to combine them by putting the wallet into the smartphone. But with a smartwatch, you don't even need to take out your smartphone. Whether it's tapping a bus card, making a card payment, or accumulating membership points, and furthermore, the risk of loss is much lower, and since it's close to the body, user authentication is also easier, so smart wallets are expected to exert the greatest power on watches.

**5) Instant camera without taking out your smartphone**
The reason cameras came into phones was for portability. The need for camera portability is constant. The desire to freely record what I see, anytime, anywhere, from Google Glass to lens-type forms, is strong enough. The ability to take a picture instantly without taking out your smartphone still has its charm, even if the performance is not as good as a smartphone. Smartphone cameras are popular not because they perform better than hybrid cameras.

**6) Outdoor Services**
Outdoor services like hiking, travel, and leisure could become popular. It would be difficult to hike or swim with a smartphone. In that sense, the need for map services will grow even more.

**7) Health Services**
A smartwatch that instantly tells you your heart rate and calls 911 if something goes wrong – this is what Samsung's Galaxy Fit will likely evolve into. Currently, it's just heart rate, but more diverse sensors like body temperature could be added in the future.

What will the future smartwatch look like, where these seven services I mentioned above come together to look the coolest? Several technical challenges still exist in this vision. Easy connectivity, precise location tracking, more diverse sensors, and the widely discussed battery life and outdoor visibility of the display are also among the current challenges for smartwatches. However, technology is continuously improving, and various attempts are being made. And now, we've started to dream of its form, and cool attempts are being made. The IoT elephant, still only partially visible, what does it look like? Will we see its outline in the second half of this year or next year? This year, Google Moto is one of my anticipated products. (I'm not very interested in health, so not Galaxy Fit...)
