Before starting this post, I'd like to show you a picture first.{{IMG:1}}It's probably one of the simplest and most famous drawings in the world. It's a drawing that came to mind when I saw a trend indicator graph of a certain website. It's an indicator graph for a service specializing in internet statistics that recorded hundreds of thousands of visitors in its first week of opening in late September, but by November, weekly visitors were below a few thousand, and page views, which exceeded millions in the first week, were only tens of thousands by November.

**1. What happened to the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport's Vworld, which once dominated trending searches?**
Not long ago, Vworld adorned portal trending searches and was praised by the media as a map surpassing "Google Earth." Vworld is a map service created by the Ministry of Land, Transport and Maritime Affairs, a government agency. You might ask, "Why?", when Naver, Daum, and Google already exist. Honestly, I want to ask the same "Why?" about this. The development cost isn't low, and these days, with the business value of maps confirmed by Google, Naver, Tmap, etc., portals and specialized companies are staking their lives on creating good services in this area.

If you search for the service concept, it's "searching for national spatial information." But from a user's perspective, how often would they need to search for national spatial information? How often would they search for nearby restaurants? Overwhelmingly, it would be the latter. It's clear that this model is destined to fail in gaining popularity as a map service.

Perhaps because of that, Vworld by the Ministry of Land, Transport and Maritime Affairs, which boasted a flashy UI featuring 3D, is virtually a dead service. After its initial launch, it succeeded in attracting millions of users, even to the point of servers crashing, due to how much marketing budget, or rather, how many articles they pushed in the media. My thought at the time was, 'They're really putting on a show of administrative performance'. Because it succeeded in attracting user attention, but it seemed like a service with no sustainability whatsoever.

**2. Services cannot succeed with bureaucracy and outsourcing.**
In reality, no company succeeds solely with great ideas and great technology. Those are just seeds; creating a proper business and a useful service is like cutting a diamond. However, the government has no experience cutting diamonds, nor does it intend to. Most government projects rely on those important diamond cutters as outsourced contractors for a certain period. Even if they cut well, they don't get anything in return, and from the government's perspective, which initiated the project, "having done it" is what matters, not success, and in fact, it shouldn't succeed. Because the government is not a business operator.

**3. Flawed planning that forgot important values and focused on visible user interfaces**
What's more absurd is that this site used a technology called Active X. Active X is an application. From a developer's perspective, Active X is used in web services to demand high privileges on the user's local machine or to create a more elaborate UI than web technologies. In this case, the latter was the purpose of use. UI is the element at the closest point of contact with the user. And a project whose purpose was to open data, or in developer terms, the "model," "national spatial information," ended up advertising, in 3D UI developer terms, "the 'view' is cool."

It's like a project to introduce a next-generation fighter jet boasting about the interior design of the cockpit instead of the jet's combat capabilities. If social is trending, they attach SNS-like features to government agency websites; if webtoons are popular, they add webtoons; if Naver looks cool, they try to configure it like a portal – they do all sorts of useless things like this. It even looks childishly flashy, like a college student's startup. The essence of a "service" is its core value. Just slapping on trendy things doesn't make it successful. If the goal is to open national spatial information, they should consider the easiest way to open that information. If it were me, I would have created the national spatial information as an Open API and provided it to portals. (Content added below on 2014-05-27) Because the customers they need to meet are there. A service that creates a website and tells customers to come find it feels quite arrogant. **ex) Daum DNA's Public APISensorQL, which I consider a successful case (Content added on 2014-05-27)** [http://dna.daum.net/tools/pg/sensorql](http://dna.daum.net/tools/pg/sensorql)​

**4. What the government should do is not internet services.**
What the government should do is not internet services. Internet services are a business domain. The very act of a national agency setting user traffic as a KPI for administrative showmanship is akin to that national agency engaging in internet business.

**5. For the sake of the market, the government should not interfere with services.**
In fact, recent developments like Shop Mail, Big Data certifications, and adult authentication for youth-harming content all strongly feel like the government is creating opportunities for specific companies. The government should not interfere with services. The government is the most powerful "authority" in South Korea, even greater than Naver or Samsung Electronics, and for that authority to meddle in internet business is something only a "communist state" would do, it's "market disruption," and it's a violation of fair trade laws in itself. I hope we no longer see the government creating internet services as administrative showmanship or favoring specific companies to the point of inconveniencing the industry.

**6. What the government should do is an invisible platform; a good platform should be lightweight and flexible.**
The government is like the nation's platform. A platform never steps forward. I don't want to see politicians who ruin the quality of services by attaching labels to supported venture companies, stating which agency supported them, thereby messing up the UI, or even appearing at IT events to interrupt speeches by famous international dignitaries just to boast about themselves. A good platform must be minimized, lightweight, and flexible to allow services running on it to operate nimbly. At the very least, it shouldn't be a service plastered with Active X, only running on IE and Windows. I sincerely hope the government becomes one that works nimbly and flexibly behind the scenes. The reason Apple made money with iTunes and was welcomed by developers was because it allowed developers to sell *their* apps, not Apple's. And the companies and people who are rewarded will recognize your hidden efforts.

**ps. 2014-05-27 Adding content regarding Vworld's Open API.**
**At the time this article was written, Vworld had already opened its Open API and developer center. My opinion is not that this part was wrong.** That part was well done; however, the regrettable point is that they only emphasized the service aspect of the 3D map. The media talked so much about the 3D map that even I, a developer, overlooked the truly important Open API. They also launched a mobile app, and surprisingly, it was receiving user tagging in a way typically done by startups. My opinion is that it would be better not to provide such user-facing services.

Additionally, I don't know how many private companies have developed using that API since then. Most of the application cases on that site are related to government agencies, and discussions about it are rarely found in developer communities. In fact, from the perspective of developers or third parties, Vworld's platform is not very attractive. The reason is that a service platform should inherently be like solid ground. However, Vworld, being government-led, is uncertain in terms of business continuity, scalability, and global support, making it unsuitable as a service platform. There's no certainty whether it could respond at the level of Google or major portals if it suddenly became a huge success like Line or Kakao while using that platform. If an internet service experiences an outage for a few days, its growth ends there. Zuckerberg, seeing cases where existing social network services failed during growth, responded with his life on the line during Facebook's growth, and that was precisely why. Responding to such a service is impossible with outsourcing and bureaucratic technical support without partnerships. Service platform providers prioritize partnerships for mutual growth, and therefore, agile response is considered the foremost virtue. However, it's naturally difficult to expect this from government projects where the operating entity and the ordering party are separate. Again, a 3D map is a service platform. The government's role is to stop at the technical platform level.
