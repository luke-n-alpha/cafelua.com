I'm finally sharing the thoughts I had about discussing the Sewol ferry incident from an IT perspective. In fact, this post is written with great caution. Ansan, where the Sewol ferry victims lived, is a familiar neighborhood where my parents currently reside, and children from one of its villages were lost in an instant. It's a neighborhood just a 15-minute drive from Sanbon, where I live. Every time I saw the news, I felt enraged, and some articles described events so tragic they made it impossible to do anything. On my social feed, I saw a post saying that any company that tries to analyze the Sewol incident through social networks to sensationalize or commercialize it is truly doing something terrible. While I fully understand these concerns, I believe the Sewol incident also offers very important implications from an IT technology perspective, and I feel compelled to share this story to move towards a better future.

**1. The Smartphones the Children Couldn't Let Go Of Until the Very End**

A characteristic of the Sewol ferry disaster was the ability to obtain incredibly vivid information from the accident scene. This included images of the accident scene captured by students and conversations through KakaoTalk. Although its authenticity hasn't been confirmed, my father heard a story in the neighborhood that all the children died clutching their cell phones. In a critical situation, the only technology connecting them to the outside world or their loved ones must have been their smartphones. When the Great East Japan Earthquake occurred in March 2011, news was still transmitted via Facebook and Twitter even in areas where the main network was cut off. It has already been reported in the media that Naver's Chairman Lee Hae-jin created Line based on this experience. However, while KakaoTalk conversations continued until the very end, the children who were having those conversations never returned. On the sinking Sewol ferry, there were hundreds of smartphones, hundreds of light sensors and GPS devices, and ultra-high-speed LTE might have been connected, but they couldn't save the children. How wonderful would it have been if the children could have escaped by riding those powerful radio waves?

**2. What will it be like in 10 years? We might face an even more horrific situation than now.**

Currently, IoT technologies, often called 'post-smartphone,' include smartwatches like Google Glass and Galaxy Gear. What will it look like in 10 years when these become widespread? Will such smart devices be able to save children then? First, looking purely at the technology, IoT devices with seamless internet connectivity, waterproof, and dustproof features will exhibit high survivability even in disaster situations. However, if the same thing happens then, and we are in a situation of total unpreparedness like now, advanced technology will show us an even greater hell than today. Parents might watch their dying children in real-time on high-resolution screens, or see the situation broadcast live on YouTube. I can imagine a horrific future where they are weeping uncontrollably in real-time while watching their dying children. In such a situation, could comments tell the children, "Get out of there now"?

**3. Let's Make Our Technologies Smart for Emergency Situations.**

I believe that the horrific future technology might bring could, on the other hand, flow in a more positive direction. For example, what if, when the ship tilted, the Coast Guard could have sent a 'evacuate the ship' message to all passengers in real-time? Or, what if, when the ship tilted or suddenly stopped, a control center or the Coast Guard could have analyzed the ship's abnormal signs and current situation and automatically notified everyone? In the world of IoT, individual smartphones and the ship they are on could communicate, exchanging information, and connect to the internet to receive external support. The 'smartness' that smartphones needed to provide to the children then was to shout, "Don't stay still, get out of there!" We need to consider why our technology was silent then, and why it only recorded the children's situation.

Of course, I know it's unreasonable to expect such smart features on a ship like the Sewol, which was over 20 years old. However, what if these technologies were mandated on ships, just as seatbelts are mandatory in cars today?

In the Sewol ferry incident, we are diligently searching for who was at fault. Of course, we are very angry at those who neglected their duties, but everyone makes mistakes. Even if they tried to fulfill their duties, someone else might have made a mistake. Although humans are said to be superior to machines, human error is always a possibility. However, no one wishes for a horrific human-made disaster that takes the lives of hundreds of children, and we must do our utmost to prevent it, no matter the cost. Therefore, to prevent this, the development of smart technology and smart disaster processes seems like a natural effort we can make going forward.

I hope that an incident like the Sewol ferry disaster never happens again in this land. Just as the Titanic disaster helped save many lives afterward, I hope that the precious children lost in the Sewol incident can protect future children.

I hope that on the Galaxy 10 held by children on a future Sewol-like ship, a single smart alarm will ring:
**"Evacuate the area."**
And I dream of a future where children return, smiling, saying it was a truly adventurous school trip.

This will be my last post about the Sewol ferry.
I offer my condolences and support to the bereaved families and survivors.
