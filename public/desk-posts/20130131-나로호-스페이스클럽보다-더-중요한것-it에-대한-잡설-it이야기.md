Naro-ho's launch was successful today. Unfortunately, I missed the live broadcast by a hair. I made sure to watch it during the Curiosity mission...

** 1. Who are the people for whom the Space Club is important?**
Last time, when I talked about Eunha-1, I discussed space shows being politically exploited.

We were anticipating being the 10th member of the Space Club, but North Korea's actions effectively made us the 11th. Whether it's a missile or not is actually a meaningless debate. It's merely a matter of how it's politically exploited.
**Anyway, whether it's the 10th or 11th is not really important. For those who cared, it was merely used to beautifully package the Naro-ho launch as an achievement under the current administration, or to mock the embarrassing result of being the 11th Space Club member, saying 'See, it wasn't a missile after all...' and to ridicule conservatives. It doesn't actually contribute anything to the true significance of Naro-ho for the Korean scientific community.**

**2. The bigger problem is the future... Investment. Investment!!**
Now, the problem isn't whether it's the 10th or 11th, but rather that we've reached a point where **how well we continue to develop space in the future is more crucial.** In fact, one of the cynical voices regarding the Space Club points out the problem that **after this launch, the next launch cannot be guaranteed.** We talk about the Korean-type launch vehicle scheduled for 2021, but that's a very distant prospect. While the Naro-ho launch is certainly a tremendous achievement, we still have a long way to go. Considering our current technological level, as we rely on Russia for 1st-stage rocket technology, our capabilities might even be lower than North Korea's, which launched on its own. That's why emphasizing satellite technology compared to North Korea is the current reality.

But is this really a lot of money?
