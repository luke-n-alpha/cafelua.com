**1. First, pay attention to your child and play with them at the restaurant? Have you ever raised a child?**
As a parent raising children myself, I'm writing this because I find this statement utterly absurd. First, you're saying children cry at restaurants because of indifference? Have you ever raised a child? The reason children struggle is because they're bored and can't play. Are you suggesting we eat our meal in 3 minutes and then spend the rest of the time playing with our children while eating? I'd like to tell you to try that yourself first to see if it's even possible. At home, not in a restaurant, when my son and daughter eat, I play and sing songs for them, and sometimes even put one or two toys on the table. Typically, I spend about 30 minutes playing with them while they eat, and then I eat my meal in 3 minutes. But is this possible in a restaurant? Should I sing songs and put toys on the table at a restaurant? While the meat is burning on the grill? Moreover, sometimes they throw toys if they don't like them. Patiently putting them back and playing with toys with them also makes them think mealtime is always playtime, just like giving them a smartphone, so I try to avoid it if possible. They throw more tantrums if I don't bring a toy they like. I strongly feel that they haven't raised a child. I'd rather ask the psychiatrist how on earth one can play with a child for 30 minutes to an hour in a Korean restaurant. That's why my spouse and I go to restaurants with playrooms whenever possible and eat in shifts. It's only possible in such cases.

**2. Steve Jobs and Silicon Valley are digital-zero? What nonsense is this?**
I don't know what kind of nonsense this is either. It is certainly true that Steve Jobs did not give his children an iPad, as stated in a New York Times article. However, he spoke of appropriate levels of control, not advocating for digital-zero.

Is coding done on paper, not computers? Are Silicon Valley leaders 'digital zero'? The effectiveness of digital technology in education has already been highly praised. I don't know what psychiatrists claim, but in the education sector, digital technology is accepted as a tool that has brought innovation to education. One such example is MOOCs. Online education is completely shaking up the very method of education. Teachers no longer conduct traditional classes. Students take individual online lessons at home, and then supplement, discuss, and interact with teachers at school to address areas where they need help. By leveraging the strengths of digital and offline education, the 50% comprehension achieved by traditional one-way classes has been boosted to 98%, and a 'failing school' that was last in the nation in the US achieved the performance of becoming one of the top schools. A student on Dokdo can now take Yale University lectures and complete them. Digital technology is a very useful tool for education, and a near-frenzy is already sweeping across the globe, causing existing universities and public education systems to ponder their future direction. Korea is also considering K-MOOCs.

**3. Unscientific Claims Advocated by the Psychiatric Community**
The psychiatric community in Korea still spouts nonsense about the frontal lobe, left brain, and right brain. Today's neuroscience tells us that the brain is not such a simple machine. The brain produces results through more complex interactions. Once, there was a strange venture claiming to maximize learning effects using some kind of brainwave, and I once asked them about it. I asked if it was the result of accumulating data on a massive scale and extracting statistically significant results. They were talking about validity with data from fewer than dozens of people. Doing something with dozens of samples in the field of pattern recognition? The attempt is meaningful, but the level is too unclear to be recognized as science. Most of the claims advocated by today's psychiatric community fall into this category. These are not results obtained by securing thousands of participants and using various control groups. On the contrary, TED features various findings suggesting that games can be beneficial for the brain. In fact, high-resolution digital is indistinguishable from analog. Analog fundamentalists believe the human brain is analog, but neural networks are fundamentally digital. Human vision is composed of cells equivalent to approximately 100 million digital pixels. Furthermore, the role of each part of the brain varies by individual and also changes with brain development. Humans don't just play games, or just exercise, or only meet their parents throughout their lives. Therefore, the claim that digital technology is harmful to humans varies greatly depending on the comparison group and the situation. It's not much different from comparing a child who played games for 12 hours out of 24 with a child who played no games at all, and then claiming that games are bad for children. Between a child who played games for 1 hour and exercised for 1 hour a day, and a child who only studied for 2 hours a day, which one would have more brain activity? I'll leave that to your imagination. Gaming is generally a more focused brain activity than studying, and it uses different parts of the brain than when exercising. Playing games is likely to involve brain activity similar to studying. Purely from a neuroscience perspective, the former child is likely to achieve better results. Of course, the child's academic performance might be poor.

**4. It's Okay to Let Kids Use Smartphones at Restaurants**
Alright. Now I'll share the conclusion that all parents want to hear, and my thoughts. **It's okay to let kids use smartphones at restaurants. However, you must make the child aware of the clear reason for showing them the smartphone.** Otherwise, they'll think it's their right to use a smartphone whenever they come to a restaurant. Using it as an appropriate incentive can lead to very good results. I teach my children that if they eat well at a restaurant and don't pick at their food, they can use a smartphone. Just as candy and chocolate aren't inherently bad, they can be a very good tool depending on how they're used. Also, **just as there are unhealthy sweets, you need to pay attention to the appropriate content you show your child.** My daughter mostly likes Tayo and Pororo, and also has a high preference for Larva, but my wife is against showing her Larva. She believes Larva, which mainly features slapstick comedy, is likely to have a negative impact on a child at this age. I somewhat agree with this. Also, we try to show TV programs with as many educational elements as possible, and we regularly watch documentaries like Cosmos together. (Actually, I like this more) We also do **education using digital tools**. Through programs introduced on code.org, I've given my daughter, who doesn't even know Korean yet, some experience, and on the tablet, I've installed Lightbot and let her play a puzzle game where she moves a robot through procedural programming. She gets frustrated with higher difficulty levels because she's still too young. From that perspective, it's clear that she doesn't just like it because it's a smartphone. A smartphone is just a shell; the software and content within are more important.

​​ **5. Digital Together Makes Children Competitive.**
Digital technology is actually a tremendous blessing for children's education. Our ancestors never had a means to access global-level education anywhere, anytime, like this. Was there ever an era where children could experience educational content created by top scholars worldwide? And children can independently collect information through internet searches, experience things, and create something. And such children change the world very quickly. It's time for us to compete with and raise our children alongside children from all over the world. The situation in South Korea, where people are steeped in exam-oriented education, thinking digital is bad for the body so let's just make them study, and coming up with absurd ideas like the shutdown law, is likely to degrade children's intellectual abilities. The status of universities, the goal of exam-oriented education, is crumbling due to MOOCs. There was even an article that Naver plans to create Next ED and promote MOOCs with KAIST. Amidst all this, 'digital zero'? Isn't it just that you dislike digital technology? When I was young, I was definitely a child who played a lot of computer games. However, I also learned more than others on the computer and created things. And now, I make a living from it. Among Korea's top 50 chaebol, excluding those who inherited, most of the newly rich were computer-savvy individuals like me, and this trend is even more pronounced overseas. Hearing the claim that Steve Jobs was anti-digital is utterly absurd. I agree that this is no longer an era to emphasize only digital. However, digital is like writing. You can't become an excellent talent just by writing, but if you can't write, it will be hard to even get close to being an excellent talent. That's the era we live in.
