코믹스브이를 창업하고, 대표를 맡은지 아직 1년이 안되가는 시점입니다. 요즘 제 블로그가 뜸하지만 IT부문에서는 글을 쓰는 블로거이면서, 네이버 경력 그리고 소프트웨어정책연구소의 연구원을 거쳐 자문이나 과제평가위원을 종종하곤 했습니다. 그리고, VR웹툰 스타트업 코믹스브이를 창업후에는 제가 오히려 피평가자로 서게 되는 일이 많아졌죠. 그러면서 이전에 보이지 않던 것들이 많이 보이게 되었습니다. 물론 소프트웨어정책연구소에서 제 메인 업무가 스타트업 생태계나 R&D평가 체계가 아니었기 때문에 그만큼 자세히 들여다 볼 기회는 없었던 것도 사실입니다.

1. 간만에 겪은 정말 칭찬할만한 평가

금일은 N정부기관의 평가를 받게 되었는데, 간만에 당락과 관계 없이 아주 만족스러운 발표와 평가를 하고 와서 그 이야기를 잠깐 드릴까 합니다. 먼저 대부분의 과제 평가에서의 분위기는 갑/을 관계가 형성됩니다. 피평가자가 을이 되고, 평가자는 갑이 되어서 모든 것을 맞추게 되죠. 평가자와 싸우지 말라는 이야기는 귀에 딱지가 않게 듣는 이야기입니다. 실제로 평가장소에 들어가면 심사위원의 대진운이 상당수 영향을 주게 마련이죠. IT라는 것이 꽤나 범위가 넓고 각 기술을 바라보는 시선들은 다 각기 다르기 마련인지라 심사위원의 커리어나 기술을 보는 관점에 따라 이해를 시키는 시간이 걸리거나 혹은 아예 선입관이 많은 경우도 쉽게 봅니다. 이 때문에 첫번째 문제는 시간이 매우 부족합니다. 요즘 대부분의 과제는 마치 IR과 같은 피칭을 요구하는것 같습니다. IR이 회사의 핵심을 전달하는데 효과적인 부분이 있기 때문에 이해는 합니다만, 문제는 IR을 하고나면 과제를 설명할 시간이 부족하고, 과제를 중심으로 설명하면 회사의 역량을 의심받기 쉽다는 것이 문제입니다. 과제심사는 이 특성을 충분히 염두해두었으면 합니다. 금일 있었던 발표의 가장 만족스러웠던 점은 충분한 시간입니다. 발표 15분, 질의 응답 15분으로 구성되어있었는데 대부분 IR은 7분정도에 맞춰지므로 나머지 5~7분을 과제를 중심으로 설명할 시간이 있었습니다. 또한 질의응답 시간의 피드백은 부족한 부분을 메꿀수 있었죠. 특히 오늘 심사의 가장 만족스러웠던 점은 진행하시는 분의 마지막 질문이었습니다. "금일 발표에서 심사위원이 오해하고 있다고 생각하는 것에 대해 이야기해주세요."  이 질문은 추가로 보너스 시간을 주고 더군다나 부족했던 의사소통을 마무리할 기회를 줍니다. 또한 더 만족스러웠던 점은 심사가 끝나고 밖에 나가니 과제 심사위원들에 대한 평가지를 주는 점이었습니다. 어느자리에 앉은 과제심사위원이 만족스러웠는지 적극적이었는지, 그리고 태도에 문제가 있는 심사위원이 있었는지. 이 설문지는 매우 중요한 의미를 내포합니다. 제가 몇개월간 과제 심사 받으며 주장했던것 중 하나가 심사위원 풀의 마련과 검증이 필요하다는 점입니다. 각 과제에 전문성 매칭 여부, 혹은 태도 등을 DB화 하고 이를 다양한 기관에서 활용할 수 있으면 좋겠다는 아이디어를 낸적이 있는데, 이러한 데이터는 이런 정책을 실현하는데 가장 기초 데이터가 되며, 심사위원으로 하여금 갑/을 관계를 벗어나 좀 더 적극적이고 친절한 자세를 고수하게 됩니다. ​

2. Ai분야의 좀 불만스러웠던 평가 경험

반대로.. 최근 있었던 좀 불만스러웠던 R&D평가사례에 대해 좀 말씀을 드리면, 이 과제가 기술개발인지 상업성인지 혹은 전문성에 대해 타겟이 어려웠던 점입니다. 특히 최근 있었던 저희 과제심사는 인공지능에 관련된 내용이었습니다. 연구개발이다보니 대게 두가지를 묻습니다. 하나는 상업성, 두번째는 기술고도화.. 그런데 저는 이 두가지 질문이 처음부터 잘 못되었다고 생각합니다. 인공지능 기술로 상업화에 성공한 사례가 있는지 첫번째 묻고 싶습니다. 또한 인공지능기술이 좋으면 그냥 잘 팔릴까요? 라고 또 묻고 싶습니다. 제 경력 중 하나는 사실 인공지능 기반의 웹서비스, 그리고 앱을 기획한적이 있습니다. 그리고 기술보증기금에서 관련 강의도 한적이 있죠.

It's been less than a year since I founded ComicsV and became its CEO. Although my blog has been quiet lately, I used to be a blogger who wrote about IT, and after working at Naver and as a researcher at the Software Policy Research Institute, I often served as an advisor or project evaluation committee member. However, after founding the VR webtoon startup ComicsV, I found myself more often on the receiving end of evaluations. This experience opened my eyes to many things I hadn't noticed before. Of course, it's also true that my main work at the Software Policy Research Institute wasn't focused on the startup ecosystem or R&D evaluation systems, so I didn't have the opportunity to look into them in such detail.

1. A Truly Commendable Evaluation I Experienced After a Long Time

Today, I underwent an evaluation by a government agency, and for the first time in a long while, regardless of the outcome, I had a very satisfying presentation and evaluation experience. I'd like to briefly share that story. First, in most project evaluations, a "갑/을" (superior/subordinate) relationship forms. The evaluated party becomes the "을" (subordinate), and the evaluator becomes the "갑" (superior), with everything conforming to their expectations. The advice "don't argue with the evaluators" is something you hear constantly. In reality, when you enter the evaluation room, the luck of the draw with the judges significantly impacts the outcome. IT is a broad field, and perspectives on each technology vary, so it often takes time to explain things depending on the judges' careers or their views on technology, or sometimes you encounter judges with strong preconceptions. Because of this, the first problem is a severe lack of time. Most projects these days seem to require a pitch similar to an IR (Investor Relations) presentation. I understand this because IR is effective in conveying the core of a company, but the problem is that after an IR, there's not enough time to explain the project, and if you focus on explaining the project, the company's capabilities are easily questioned. I hope project evaluations take this characteristic into sufficient consideration. The most satisfying aspect of today's presentation was the ample time. It was structured with 15 minutes for presentation and 15 minutes for Q&A. Since most IRs are tailored to about 7 minutes, I had an additional 5-7 minutes to explain the project in detail. Also, the feedback during the Q&A session helped fill in the missing parts. In particular, the most satisfying part of today's evaluation was the final question from the facilitator: "Please tell us if there's anything you believe the judges misunderstood in today's presentation." This question provided additional bonus time and, moreover, an opportunity to conclude any insufficient communication. What was even more satisfying was that after the evaluation, when I stepped outside, I was given evaluation forms for the project judges. It asked which judge was satisfactory, who was proactive, and if there were any judges with problematic attitudes. This questionnaire holds very significant meaning. One of the things I've advocated for during several months of project evaluations is the need to establish and verify a pool of judges. I once suggested the idea of creating a database of judges' expertise matching each project, their attitudes, etc., and making it available for various institutions to use. Such data would be the fundamental basis for realizing such policies and would encourage judges to maintain a more proactive and friendly demeanor, moving beyond the "갑/을" relationship.

2. A Somewhat Unsatisfying Evaluation Experience in the AI Field

Conversely, regarding a somewhat unsatisfying R&D evaluation experience I recently had, the difficulty lay in targeting whether the project was about technology development, commercial viability, or expertise. In particular, our recent project evaluation was related to artificial intelligence. Since it's R&D, two things are usually asked: first, commercial viability, and second, technological advancement. However, I believe these two questions are fundamentally flawed from the start. I'd like to ask, first, if there are any successful commercialization cases using AI technology. And second, I'd like to ask if good AI technology simply sells well. One of my career experiences is actually having planned AI-based web services and apps. I've also given related lectures at the Korea Technology Finance Corporation.

                                [**[Lecture Open] Understanding AI Technology and Services** Lecture materials from the "KIAT & Korea Technology Finance Corporation hosted, 5 New Business Technology Trend Education Course" on September 21, 2016... fstory97.blog.me](http://fstory97.blog.me/220934750379)

First, regarding commercial viability, it takes quite a long time for AI to reach a commercial level. And what truly makes it valuable is not so much the technical skill as it is the technology planning. There's always talk about marketing and such... but poorly planned products won't sell even with marketing, and I don't understand the intention behind asking us to develop and sell something in a year-long R&D project as if we're cooking beans in a flash, especially when it's not even a startup project. Also, the most challenging aspect of AI technology research is not the algorithm but the data. What's most frustrating is that I've never seen an evaluator ask about data. I also found it frustrating that commercial potential was continuously discussed based on technical capability. The characteristic of AI research is that anyone can easily reach 95%, but reaching 98% or 99% is difficult, and in fact, the most important thing is that with good technology planning, 95% can be sufficient. A well-crafted service scenario can cover the rest in the time it would take to strive for 99%. Furthermore, to create such a well-crafted service scenario, data and user scenarios must align. However, I haven't seen many evaluators who understand this. I thought it might be because it's R&D, but then, on the other hand, it also seemed strange that from a commercial perspective, everyone talked about the company's revenue level and technical capabilities. If a company's revenue level is very high, it wouldn't participate in such evaluations and wouldn't engage in industry-academia collaboration in the first place. Also, from a company's perspective, it involves the leakage of core technology, which requires a longer period of trust. The R&D in the AI field that I would like to propose should focus on "**data**," "**technology planning capability**," and "**appropriateness of personnel**," and **technology and commercial viability should be viewed from an "investment" perspective**.

Honestly, AI won't generate immediate money. If you want to make money, I'd advise investing in blockchain and doing blockchain R&D. However, I personally believe that AI is the field where academia should truly conduct R&D. Companies do what makes money. Academia, no matter how much they try with things like blockchain, will never beat the industry. Companies have more money and more, better personnel. Postdocs go to such corporate research labs, their salaries are incomparable to those of master's and doctoral students in academia, and they develop things faster with more support in much more systematic development environments. But on the other hand, AI is a very burdensome research area for companies. That's why they push harder. That's what makes AI research difficult in the industry. Commercial viability is unclear, developer labor costs and infrastructure costs are high, but it definitely seems like it will work... so they have to invest, and labor costs are expensive, but should they send these nerds who can't communicate to urgent service projects just because they're doing R&D??? They face such temptations. (Oh, I'm not talking about myself...) Wealthy companies would go all in, but startups and companies like ours recognize the necessity but don't have the luxury of doing it with our own personnel. Conversely, academia has the personnel, time, and infrastructure for research, but they are mostly zero in terms of data and technology planning. Good technology alone won't get you anywhere. **I wonder if those who evaluate technology could also approach it from a learning perspective.** I believe I've had a relatively diverse service experience, but I don't know much once it deviates slightly from what I've done. I also think that what I've done is almost past its expiration date, roughly 3-5 years. (This is also why I left the research institution where I worked for 3 years and started a company.) I merely infer based on my field. Anyway, I don't know everything, and I'm not an immense expert, but my opinion based on my experience is this: good evaluations create good projects and are the most important part that can greatly enhance the effectiveness of policies. It's been a while since I posted anything on my blog.
