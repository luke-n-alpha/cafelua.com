Since we can't know Google's true intentions unless we're Google employees, I'd like to offer my own speculative thoughts on **this topic (Google discontinuing Daydream)**. To understand Google, one must start by defining its projects. The article states that the "**Google Daydream VR project began with the ambition to gather VR video content into one place to create the 'VR version of YouTube.'**" However, Google already has a VR version of YouTube; it's just YouTube. Currently, 360-degree videos are the most widely distributed, and it's also the most popular app on Facebook and other VR platforms. The method of linking VR apps and mobile app accounts via URLs is also a characteristic, representing a typical n-screen strategy. To understand Daydream, it's more appropriate to look at the Pixel phone. Google's Pixel phone is Google's reference phone. Starting with Nexus, Google's benchmark phones are not at the top of the Android ecosystem. Nevertheless, they continue to develop it, showcasing the highest level of technology and being compared to other manufacturers' phones. However, looking at the hardware, it's always not pretty... clunky... and it's not like the camera is the best, or the performance is the best. The only good points are advantages in service integration, such as unlimited Google Photos. It never surpasses Samsung or Huawei. Google has always made such devices. Now, let's return to VR. What's the biggest difference when comparing the VR ecosystem to the smartphone ecosystem? First, Apple is absent, and instead, Facebook is present. So, what's the difference for Google between Apple in smartphones and Facebook in VR? Apple's ecosystem is characterized by a vertical platform extending from its software platforms like OSX and iOS to the App Store and iPhone. As a counter-strategy, Google built an Android alliance by supplying Android OS and integrated Google services to manufacturers. The launch of the Pixel phone is akin to a technology demonstration for this Android alliance. What about Oculus, or rather, Facebook? Facebook's Oculus Rift runs on Windows OS. And Oculus Go and Quest run on Android. This is where Google's biggest difference in stance inevitably arises. Facebook is not Google's competitor. Its SNS domain failed spectacularly with Google Plus, and while Facebook's video content has grown rapidly, the heavy content suitable for VR is found on YouTube and Netflix. These are currently the top apps in VR. In other words, Google has no reason to compete with Oculus. Oculus's solution for VR expansion is, in fact, a collaboration with Google using Android. The company that would find this inconvenient is Apple. Furthermore, one of the recent growth areas is the rapid rise of All-in-One (AIO) type VR HMD devices. This would seem like the optimal time for Daydream, so why discontinue it? Companies making AIO devices currently include HTC, Xiaomi (Facebook), Pico, and DPVR. All of them use Android. **Google views VR as an extension of the Android ecosystem.**

And there's one thing we learned through Daydream: an online-based, integrated B2C market is still premature. I, too, initially thought VR would have a single platform like mobile, with global app stores emerging. But there hasn't been a killer app with that level of influence or device completeness yet. Instead, VR devices are placed offline in locations appropriate for each site. For example, a dinosaur VR app is available in a dinosaur exhibit, and a Stone Age VR app in a Stone Age exhibit. So, don't jump to conclusions that it won't work like mobile. **This is actually similar to the early days of PCs, which were B2B-centric. PCs were like that before Microsoft hit it big with Office. (In fact, mobile also had a B2B sector even before the iPhone. There's almost a 10-year gap from the early WinCE versions to the iPhone.) It infiltrated B2B and various industrial sectors, then exploded when killer apps emerged and technology reached a critical point.** In that regard, some people are enthusiastic about Apple's AR glasses, but I'm very pessimistic. Apple will still dream of an integrated market, and Apple's AR glasses, at best, will be like the Apple Watch. They won't go beyond that. Of course, like the iPad, they might succeed in creating a separate niche in B2B. Because they're Apple. Apple's will be cooler than Epson's or Samsung's. But the ecosystem will be smaller than Apple TV. Instead, Apple's mobile SDK, ARKit, will likely have high utility, as it's a sub-component of the mobile ecosystem. However, Google's Android will have a much greater advantage in terms of AR glasses. Android is already used everywhere in daily life, in the embedded system domain where Windows was once used. Of course, we won't know if it's Google's technology. It might bear the Epson, Samsung Electronics, or LG Electronics mark, and people will use it for work because their boss told them to, rather than wanting to. Instructions will even come down via AR...

Webtoons have a structure where if one person works hard for a week, 10 million readers are happy for 3 minutes. If all users consume all content, then 20 people working hard can produce 1 hour of content. (This is hard to imagine in an MMORPG.) **Currently, many views on VR webtoons focus on high-quality IP and "Wow" experiences, but the true strength of webtoons lies in their ability to produce new content cheaply and quickly. The webtoons currently released in Korean by ComicsV are 100% original content, totaling over 100 episodes. In terms of video running time, this exceeds 300 minutes. Production costs are the same as regular webtoons (paid as royalties or MG), and the artists produced them directly.** Of course, since the market itself is not yet large, it may take quite some time for this ecosystem to function properly, but **I believe VR webtoons are one of the optimal answers to the "continuous new content production" challenge in VR.** There are always 100 reasons why something won't work.
