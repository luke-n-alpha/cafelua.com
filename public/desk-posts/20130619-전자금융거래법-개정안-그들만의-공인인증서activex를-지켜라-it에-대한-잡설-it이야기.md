**1. The Trite Controversy Resurfacing: Accredited Certificates and ActiveX****{{IMG:1}}
****[Electronic Financial Transaction Act Amendment]**Accredited Certificates and ActiveX are, in fact, technologies from different layers, but they seem to be treated the same. In reality, ActiveX has many technical vulnerabilities and goes against the philosophy of the web. Internet services that use ActiveX sacrifice the advantages of internet services for the benefits of applications, and it's a method that increases costs. In the past, the reason accredited certificates adopted ActiveX technology was partly due to the technical circumstances of the time.

**2. Re-evaluating Accredited Certificates and ActiveX by Today's Standards?**
However, what about today's internet commerce and banking environment? Beyond payment methods using accredited certificates and ActiveX, various other technical means have emerged since then. As mentioned earlier, ActiveX is a method that sacrifices many aspects for the unique advantages that applications (apps) possess. Furthermore, due to its technical characteristics, ActiveX also has the effect of significantly increasing security vulnerabilities, which is a drawback of the web. For this reason, even Microsoft made efforts to phase out ActiveX, and today, in most internet services, ActiveX and plugins have become technologies that are absolutely never used unless absolutely!!!!! necessary, due to installation hurdles, security vulnerabilities, and development resources.

**In short, if I were to describe the current internet banking system from an engineer's perspective, it is a system that encompasses both the disadvantages of apps and the web, in an attempt to gain the security advantages of past apps. Costs are high, usage is inconvenient, and its initial purpose of security is continuously being questioned.**

Therefore, in today's ICT industry, ActiveX is practically branded as public enemy number one, a technology that must be removed to vitalize mobile platforms and e-commerce. And for this reason, under the current government, there are movements to reform the legal systems concerning ActiveX and accredited certificates. This is a very natural technological trend.

**3. Yet, there are those who insist on keeping them.**
Surprisingly, however, there are also those who oppose this.
{{IMG:2}}
[http://www.ktnet.co.kr/~cathesis/anti_opennet.html](http://www.ktnet.co.kr/~cathesis/anti_opennet.html)
They might be people from related industries, or those who benefited from the existing platform. As an editorial from Electronic Times appeared on my feed, industry professionals passionately debated. Regardless, while they once again emphasize the need for security, which was the initial purpose of its introduction, it's also true that they are not receiving much empathy for various reasons. Above all, there is a strong feeling of a cartel.

As I pointed out in a previous post, the name "accredited" (공인) can simultaneously acquire infinite "reliability" and be used as a means for the state to evade responsibility. The moment the "accredited" name is removed, there's a possibility that more responsibility will fall upon banks and payment systems. This means the state will no longer have an obligation to defend them. And, in fact, these are already private companies, and the public nature of banks has largely diluted since the IMF crisis. It is unacceptable for a private company that pursues profit rather than public activity to pass responsibility to the state.

I naturally believe we should go in this direction, but it seems there are others who think differently. The idea of pursuing profit while still receiving state protection is not suitable for today's market capitalism.

**4. The core of the problem is that designating constantly changing technology by law is itself the problem.**
**- It's not a technology problem, but a responsibility problem.**
This is what I believe to be the core of the problem. It's time for the state to reduce its interference in businesses. Business activities have become too complex and vast for the state to interfere with, and the pace of technological development is rapid. In the past, in a capitalist system where financial and payment systems were viewed simply from the perspective of savings and consumption, the responsibility for currency was entirely within the public domain. However, with the strengthening of capitalist mechanisms like loans and investments, the realm of private enterprise in South Korea's capitalism is increasingly expanding today. It's similar to how a venture company, where the CEO controls everything and inter-departmental unity is emphasized, grows into a global enterprise, requiring delegation of many areas and encouraging competition among organizations. And what needs to be done then is not detailed plans, but strategy, oversight, and rule-setting.

If the state mandates that payments over 300,000 won must use a specific ActiveX technology, isn't that already excessive interference? The lifecycle of technology is not long enough to be enshrined in law. What's important is the responsibility for security, the prevention of financial transaction damages, and the process in case of damage. What the state should do is not provide detailed guides for companies, but set rules and strictly hold accountable those who violate rules or cause accidents. This approach is much more important from a social perspective than a technical one.
