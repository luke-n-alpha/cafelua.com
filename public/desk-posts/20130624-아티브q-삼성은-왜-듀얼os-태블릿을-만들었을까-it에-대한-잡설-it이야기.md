Recently, Samsung Electronics released a product called Ativ Q. Its most notable feature was the dual OS, allowing simultaneous use of Android and Windows 8. Technically, it's not a true dual OS with separate bootstraps, but rather Android running as a VM on top of Windows. This seems possible because the device uses Intel's i-series Haswell processor, offering overwhelming hardware performance compared to ARM-based Android devices, allowing sufficient performance through virtualization alone. The downside, however, is that it's incredibly expensive compared to Android tablets. When you think about it, it's quite a strange device. Why would they bother to cram the lightweight Android onto a device that uses a powerful Haswell processor just to run Windows 8?! While advertised as a new innovation, I believe there's a very clever Samsung strategy hidden beneath. I'd like to talk about that.

**1. Previous Case: RIM Running Android on Another OS**
In fact, such efforts have been made before. RIM's PlayBook, for instance, attempted to run Android apps through virtualization. Why did they choose such a cumbersome method? The reason was the lack of killer applications on RIM's new tablet platform and the limited number of apps, making tablet utilization difficult. The solution adopted to compensate for the lack of 3rd-party support was OS-level compatibility. And a strategy identical to this seems to be replayed in the Ativ Q. In other words, the most fundamental reason for the Ativ Q's dual OS strategy is the lack of a strong app lineup for Windows 8. For hardware to sell well, it needs to be supported by sufficient software power, and it seems Samsung's partner, Microsoft's platform, is not adequately fulfilling that role.

**2. Samsung's Intention: Can't Miss Out on Both Windows and Android.**
It's a well-known fact that Samsung Electronics pursues a multi-platform strategy. The biggest change coming to the tablet market in 2013 will be the showdown between Windows and Android. For Samsung Electronics, it would be ideal to play a game where they have no burden, regardless of who wins. Having a foot in both camps allows them to react sensitively to market changes and reduce their dependence on and influence from OS providers. Tizen is also said to be a means to check Google's influence, and I agree with that. This dual OS also appears to be the same strategy. It's a strategic move to both check and support both Microsoft and Google. It's undeniably a very shrewd play. Furthermore, this game is low-risk because Windows was something they had to do as a PC manufacturer anyway, and Android can be obtained from Google without significant cost burden. Of course, this is relative, and porting and maintenance costs are not insignificant.

**3. Ativ Q and Ativ 3 are Transitional Models for Windows Tablets**
Samsung Electronics' stance still seems to be exploratory. Personally, I believe Clover Trail opened the possibility for Windows 8 tablets, and its successor, Bay Trail, will be the platform that truly takes off. For use as a tablet device, Haswell has too short battery life, and Clover Trail has clear limitations in resolution and RAM. Windows 8 is also scheduled for major revisions in 8.1. Therefore, the Ativ Q and Ativ 3 are still transitional models for Windows tablets. Having Android to fill that gap was, therefore, a decent choice. A hybrid device is fundamentally an unconventional model in a transitional technological phase, designed to leverage the advantages of both sides during a period of technological change. In that sense, Samsung's dual OS strategy indicates that Windows tablets are still in a transitional phase.
