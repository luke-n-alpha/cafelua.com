I tried to explain the 'human effort' aspect as thoroughly and honestly as possible. However, the decisive reason for the registration being rejected was as follows:

**“Prompt input cannot be recognized as an 'editorial work' or 'artistic work' as 'human effort'.”**

This statement has also been confirmed in an interview with the Director of Copyright Policy at the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism's Copyright Bureau.

Personally, I somewhat agree. Simple prompt input may be difficult to copyright because it results in **random image outputs**. However, through this registration rejection, I directly confirmed **the blind spot in copyright interpretation in the AI era**.

What most companies want is not 'copyright protection as an artwork'. It is **copyright protection for compositional elements such as characters, settings, and stories**. And the subject is often a 'company,' not an individual artist. This trend is particularly evident in **digital content-centric industries** like games and webtoons. From a company's perspective, **AI is merely a tool**, and the focus is on **capital's execution power** rather than the pain of creation. While some might view this commercialism as undermining copyright as art, **the actual foundation of today's cultural industry is capital's planning and execution**.

Nevertheless, observing the approach of this copyright law, I realized how much it is centered on 'artists' and 'creative labor'. Of course, I do not intend to disparage the value of artists and creative labor. Rather, I believe **continuous budget support is needed** for this field. However, the problem is a **lack of vision regarding 'capital's effort' and 'IP copyright'**. Capital employs the efforts of creators. This is an industrially valid approach. If the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism does not recognize this structure, I believe it would be more appropriate for **AI copyright to be transferred to the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy or the Ministry of Science and ICT**.

This argument might seem somewhat radical.

However, in the world of digital works, **all creation is ultimately reduced to 'numbers'**.
- A prompt is not a 'command' but **an array of tokens**.
- Image guides are vectors.
- Whether it's music or video, AI understands it through a multimodal engine.

Everything input into AI is a **quantified signal**, and human creations are transmitted to AI in this manner. And that AI has now become an entity that can handle **beyond text responses, including video and audio**. Even two years ago, I would have found it difficult to make such an argument.

But now it's different. **Since ChatGPT, AI has advanced beyond imagination**. Google's Veo3 creates movie-like videos with just a prompt.

Now, AI is not just a machine that answers; it is an **employee and a colleague**. The development tool currently being built is also being developed with such a concept. But if a CEO comes up with an idea and an employee creates content, and the company cannot own the copyright, is that truly the right direction? There was a similar concept in an IITP government project; would all content created with that tool then not be recognized for copyright? In my view, the current AI copyright system has such an **unrealistic structure**.

This post is **not intended to criticize the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism or copyright officials.** AI technology is changing too rapidly, and while Korea is still debating 'copyright eligibility,' **overseas platforms and companies are already churning out AI content.** In the meantime, **Korean AI content creators and companies are likely to remain unprotected**. Of course, one could say, "Just draw it yourself and submit it." While not that difficult, **that is an unnecessary cost and a self-imposed regulation**. I hope that an **independent IP copyright system for AI content** will be established separately. There is no time.

I have been advocating for the establishment of an AI copyright system centered on creator collaboration **since I founded ComicsV, seven years ago**. However, that period of validity has passed, and the situation has changed, leaving us with nothing we can do. We were complacent, thinking it was a distant future, and this is what happened. This is because the US and China are already **disregarding creator-centric systems** for AI copyright and openly adopting policies that favor AI companies. As a result, in the US, a government official who advocated for protecting creators' copyrights was even **dismissed**. By the second half of this year, **AI content will explode, but South Korea is stuck in copyright discussions at the ChatGPT 3.5 level. Meanwhile, AI technology has undergone a seismic shift, making it difficult to keep up.**
