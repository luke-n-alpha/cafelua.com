**1. Suneung, South Korea's Biggest Event: A Nation of Fierce Competition**
I only realized today was Suneung (CSAT) day after seeing mobile news on my way to work. Suneung, one of South Korea's biggest events, is not just a major occasion for high school seniors and families with children preparing for college entrance exams. Police become busy, company commutes are delayed, and even airplanes are grounded, showing a situation akin to a national emergency for all citizens. Even civil defense drills, which prepare for actual combat, seem less combative compared to Suneung day. Everyone offers words of encouragement for the Suneung. "Do well on the Suneung." "Cheer up." Students who excel in their studies will be nervous, while those who struggle might feel like they're being dragged to a slaughterhouse. And when the results are announced, there will be those who rejoice and those who sigh, perhaps preparing for another year. Was the ancient civil service exam similar to this? It seems not much different from that time when it was considered the only path to success and honor. Good universities have become like a religion, and South Korea, which values connections, has solidified this, and its power remains immense. They even say that Seoul National University and KAIST dominate everything in the IT industry, which is supposedly merit-based, so that says it all.

**2. Let's Reconsider the Value of Education. The Value of Education is Knowledge Transfer.**
Now, let's talk about current education. It's certainly no exaggeration to say that education culminates in the Suneung, but I question whether today's education truly fulfills its role. First, why do we take the Suneung? Why do we have to study? There's even a webtoon based on these questions:

"I want to marry a celebrity." (Celebrity fan)

Indeed, today's education system will elicit different answers from each stakeholder. Education is, in fact, a massive industry, and the nation, corporations, students, and parents will all have different answers.

However, if we talk about the essence of education, it is, in fact, **"knowledge transfer."** As the power of knowledge became power and money, the importance of knowledge transfer, or education, gained value. In other words, the value of education comes from the power of "knowledge." This knowledge can, of course, include character.

**3. Suneung, Composed of Core Subjects (Korean, English, Math): Why Line Up Based on Basic Knowledge?**
As I mentioned above, I said that the power of knowledge is the value of education. Does the Suneung, created to prove this knowledge acquisition, effectively convey this core value? To do well on the Suneung, you still need to excel in Korean, English, and Math. And these are called "basic subjects." But isn't that a bit strange? The idea that doing well on an exam composed of "basic subjects" holds value, in other words, doing well on the Suneung only proves that a child is good at the "basics." To have value, one must add something beyond these basics.

However, I feel that we are staking too much on these basics, and the value of universities entered through these basics is also excessively high. Personally, I believe the difficulty of the Suneung should be eased, and if the Suneung truly consists of "basic" subjects, it should be a criterion for eliminating candidates, not for selecting them. And in fact, in businesses and practical fields, the standards of Korean, English, and Math are used as criteria for eliminating candidates (civil service exams being an exception).

**4. Suneung, It Is Nothing.**
Let me say it again. Suneung is not about whether you do well or poorly. It's okay to not do so well on the Suneung. Not all brilliant individuals who create highly complex things are good at mental arithmetic. Of course, statistically, they might be better than the average person, because an excellent brain also includes memorization skills. But if we look at overall brain ability, an IQ test is more effective than the Suneung. IQ tests were designed for that purpose.

**5. Is University Really Important?**
Now that we've talked about Suneung, let's return to universities. Universities have set departments and curricula. And these curricula are developed based on academics and industry. They say that if you complete this course, you can succeed in that field. I once heard from a professor that our seniors considered getting a job at the Ministry of Communications (post office) to be the highest achievement. This then led to Korea Telecom (wired communication), then more recently to wireless communication companies like mobile carriers, and nowadays, internet companies are also preferred. Industry changes constantly like this. Universities also change their curricula accordingly, but as the pace of industrial change accelerates, the curriculum might even change between when you enroll and when you graduate. This phenomenon is not limited to a single department. More broadly, there are cases where industry needs shift from hardware to software, meaning from electrical engineering to computer software.

This means there's no guarantee that a popular major when you enroll will still be popular when you graduate, or when you reach old age. Therefore, while the university and major are certainly very important, the direction you set now won't be your final goal in life.

**6. Nevertheless, the Value of University Lies in Youth**
So, I'm not saying that Suneung and university have no value at all. There is definitely value in university. It's highly likely that your university life will be a period where you meet new people on campus, find friends who share similar perspectives, try things together, wonder if this path is right or that path is right, wander, worry, and experience trial and error.

Because it is the only period in South Korea that does not teach "competition." In fact, this is another result that indicates flaws in South Korea's education system, but from elementary school through high school, and then after graduating college and getting a job, South Korea has been teaching "competition" for decades, and that is the current reality of South Korea. Of course, some people now say that universities also involve competition. However, I believe the value of university still lies in youth and freedom. And even if you don't go to university, your early twenties should be a period where you can enjoy that. Because young people have just begun their lives.

**7. Education in an Unpredictable Future**
This post started with the intention of discussing changes in the future education system, but the conclusion has veered too much into university and Suneung talk. In fact, the education system will change. And what is certain is that the current Suneung and universities will lose their competitiveness. This is something I can guarantee. The reason is that knowledge changes faster than curricula, and academia will exhibit an organic pattern of frequent convergence and divergence. The College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) might attempt to structure itself in a way that enhances problem-solving abilities in preparation for such an era, but what is clear is that such problem-solving abilities will not be assessed by exams or taught in cram schools, but will largely depend on more experiential and intuitive aspects. Because computers already excel at memory, and that memory (information) can be easily accessed anywhere, the aspects that will be considered more important for humans will be these experiential, intuitive, and analytical abilities.

Finally, let me tell you. The College Scholastic Ability Test. It is nothing.
