**1. The Past and Limitations of AI**
However, AI that plays Go or AI conversational bots are not new. From a service perspective, not a technical one, AI that plays Go is closer to a sophisticated NPC game machine. Conversational bots like Max in the PC era and SimSimi in the mobile era have already appeared in Korea.

However, the reason these could not become truly meaningful services was the difficulty in creating human-level AI. Simple speech recognition or individual context recognition each developed separately. Computing resources were limited, and there were individual situations where if one thing didn't work, another would have problems. Although the situation has improved with the development of computing technology, humans still process an enormous amount of unstructured data in real-time, including visual, auditory, contextual information, and past experiences, making AlphaGo's 160,000 game records seem trivial. Furthermore, experiences etched into genes through tens of millions of years of evolution, combined with decades of life experiences, amount to an enormous volume if converted into data. Unlike the public's misunderstanding that AI in computer science means creating a human with a computer, its true meaning lies in a machine solving problems it couldn't solve before, like a human, by finding approximate values for a specific purpose. Among these, conversation is a very high-difficulty problem, actually more difficult than Go. Ordinary people like us cannot beat a machine that defeats Lee Sedol at Go, but we can argue better than AlphaGo. This is because conversation is actually one of the most advanced brain functions, and we only know of one machine that has just passed the Turing test in an unconventional way. Clearly, it seems we are passing a certain point.

**2. So, who can do it well?**
So, who can do it well? Will an AI startup with geniuses suddenly pop out an amazing search engine like Wolfram Alpha? Geniuses always surpass the thoughts of ordinary people, but in my humble opinion, only companies with massive amounts of user data can excel at AI bots. Even if there are thousands of supercomputers, they are useless without data to train them. Furthermore, the training data must have at least minimal tagging or related information suitable for training. In social network services, this could be relationships between people, or links embedded in tweets. In the case of search services, it's queries and the results clicked by users. To discuss and develop AI services, the major premise is that a platform capable of collecting user data must exist. Or at least, collect and release it for research purposes. I believe this is why government-led initiatives, simply pouring money, cannot solve the problem.

**3. What about South Korea?**
Fortunately, in Korea's case, I think the situation is a bit better. Because there are Naver and Kakao, which created Line, as platform companies leading user data. However, it's difficult to be optimistic due to the fact that 70% of the world uses Google, Facebook, and WhatsApp. It's hard to expect to become a global company by analyzing only Korean. AI is not just a technology; its final product is data created by society, AI software technology, and computer hardware. I believe the difficulty order is society, software, hardware. This is why I believe AI needs to be viewed with a longer breath. An approach like suddenly building some hardware and expecting results in a few years can quickly extinguish the nascent spark of AI. I hope people don't have high expectations for Facebook's bots either. Personally, I don't think it will be much different from Line's "official accounts" that used to answer weather or fortune-telling questions conversationally.
