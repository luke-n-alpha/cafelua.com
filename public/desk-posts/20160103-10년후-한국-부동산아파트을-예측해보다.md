Soaring *jeonse* prices, and the fear of falling prices in the sales market. Currently, it's hard to predict the direction of the real estate market in Korea, especially for apartments. Amidst this uncertainty, I've been thinking about how future housing forms might change. In the short term, I really don't know if prices will go up or down. Especially since the population began to decline in 2019, the implications are an unprecedented change, so I don't know how things will evolve. While one might predict a decline based on overseas examples, I hesitate to make such claims given how differently real estate markets operate across countries.

**1. Real Estate in the Era of O2O and On-Demand**
Recent changes in the IT sector include O2O (Online-to-Offline) and IoT (Internet of Things). Online services, or more precisely, information services, are creating distribution innovation and convergence/divergence in various offline sectors. Some key trends here include a shift from ownership to usage. Car-sharing is a good simple example. We can also call this convergence or disruption. Coupang and Amazon are attempting synergy or vertical integration by combining commerce with distribution. I believe these attempts at change actually started with Apple's iTunes. What kind of model would emerge if we applied on-demand and disruption to real estate? Real estate platform companies might emerge, displacing small-scale real estate agencies, and these companies might collaborate with or even own large-scale real estate suppliers. What if "Naver Apartments" or "Airbnb Apartments" came into existence? Such apartments might not offer sales at all. They would likely offer pricing that reflects demand in real-time at an appropriate level.

**2. Real Estate's Transformation into a Service**
Service enhancement due to disruption will also be noticeable. For local commercial districts and educational infrastructure, apartment residents are customers. Efforts to maximize the value for these customers will emerge through various O2O services and be linked with apartment infrastructure. The reason people live in apartments rather than detached houses is often due to good infrastructure. Currently, apartments tend to build everything internally, forming a silo-like structure. However, by creating an apartment platform model that more actively connects and communicates with the local community, it might be possible to provide higher services at a lower cost.

**3. Apartments as Part of the Local Community**
The concept of a sharing economy will draw apartment residents, who have been somewhat isolated, outwards. By sharing their surplus, costs can be lowered, and by sharing their talents to teach children, the value of the local community can be enhanced. It is also expected to evolve into a form where residents can act as both consumers and providers within local commercial districts and educational infrastructure. Combined with fintech, mileage provided by apartment platforms might be usable within the local community.

**4. Emergence of Platform Companies Innovating Housing Costs with Large-Scale Information Platforms and Capital Investment**
For the aforementioned forms to operate ideally, a large amount of capital would likely be required. While maintenance costs might be cheaper and more efficient in some ways, to integrate the already siloed areas of local communities and the existing construction and real estate industries, a company with significant capabilities would need to step in. For example, a company capable of buying an entire apartment complex in Gangnam. Nevertheless, like all platform companies, they would innovate housing costs. There could be ways for startups to propose models and grow in new cities, or for companies like Kakao, Naver, or even grown-up Yellow Mobile or Zigbang to create such models. What's clear is that real estate transaction information will not be as opaque as it is now, and corporate real estate platforms will increase. However, looking at the current New Stay (public rental housing), I only think it's still a long way off... Wirye is practically a sale, and Suwon and Dongtan have poor locations, making me wonder, "At this price, here?"

**5. In 10 Years, Apartments Will No Longer Be Objects to Buy and Sell.**
I wonder if, in 10 years, an era will unfold where people choose homes to move into. It will be an era of choosing to move in, not choosing to buy. Sales, I think, might lean more towards village-style detached houses. Frankly, the reason people want to own apartments is not out of affection for the apartment itself, but largely due to the surrounding infrastructure or investment sentiment. Investment sentiment will disappear when apartments shift to a sharing concept and corporate rentals emerge (of course, if you have about 500 billion won, you can just do corporate rentals yourself). Affection for infrastructure is sufficiently met by renting. However, detached houses, with their significant potential for personal customization, are better suited for the desire for ownership.

In conclusion, in the future, owning a home as an individual will likely only be possible for those who have achieved significant success. They might own a splendid town-style house, or a real estate company. Instead, most will live in rental properties. And what will be slightly different from the traditional landlord-tenant relationship is that residents will be customers and partners in creating the platform. Because community management is important... perhaps even preferential entry might be granted based on academic background or profession.

While construction companies might be best suited for this kind of business, it seems difficult due to their corporate culture. Instead, large corporations like Samsung or LG, which have electronics or software businesses, or companies like Naver or Kakao, might be able to undertake such ventures. The problem is that current Korean laws might also need to be reformed.
