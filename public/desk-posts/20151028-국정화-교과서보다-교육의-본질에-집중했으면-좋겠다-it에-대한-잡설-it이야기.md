The discussion about state-authored textbooks continues endlessly. Recently, however, I read an article that questioned the very essence of "Korean history." Reading it made my head spin. It felt like the pieces of everything I had learned were finally falling into place.

The history subjects we learn are broadly divided into two: world history and national history. However, when we learn science, we learn the history of science; when we learn math, the history of math; when we learn music, the history of music; and when we learn art, the history of art, at least lightly. When I connected these things, I wondered, "Why didn't I think of that before?" In other words, it's not that we haven't learned it. The 1996 College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) I saw was a shock itself. There was a passage about land in the national history section. This type of problem, which I had never seen before, flustered me. As a result, the CSAT taken in 1996 recorded the lowest scores in the history of the CSAT. At the time, students and parents were furious at these new types of problems that rendered countless efforts of high school seniors useless. Consequently, the difficulty of the 1997 CSAT was lowered.

But looking back, when I worked as a planner in a company, all the problems I encountered were full of these kinds of issues. Wanting to enter a new field, looking at overseas cases and wondering how to apply them to domestic situations, identifying our problems, and figuring out how to improve them. I connected disparate facts, analyzed them, created alternatives, and implemented those alternatives. Many of these endeavors sometimes led to success and sometimes to failure, but there was no absolute success or absolute failure. Success had to be refined, and failure had to be learned from. No knowledge was independent; everything influenced each other, and in fact, there were no boundaries. There were only different results from different perspectives. And the work of organizing those perspectives and coordinating different opinions – that was precisely the problem-solving process. As someone from a software engineering background, I was quite familiar with handling numbers (data), and since our work was in the internet, or rather, the software field, it seemed to be a strong advantage as a specialist that it was very easy to integrate.

Discussions about SW education, when delved into in detail, are integrated into SW utilization covered in practical arts and physical computing in technology subjects. Furthermore, SW naturally originated from mathematics. It seems that establishing its place by defining common ground with existing subjects is not an easy task. In programming terms, it's like having legacy code and adding new features, isn't it? Moreover, due to the computing resources used by legacy code, resources for implementing new features are severely lacking. Many new systems or initiatives in Korea find it difficult to apply new things within existing frameworks, which is quite similar to this situation.

In this case, in programming, there are two methods. First is the method most preferred by programmers: creating something new. This involves building anew based on the essential requirements that are superficially apparent, without analyzing existing details. It's also easy to apply the latest techniques, and there's no need to worry about existing history. However, if that's not possible, the structure of the existing code is changed. Simply put, it's undertaking innovation. I believe innovation isn't about coming up with something cool and new, but about breaking down outdated practices. It's a very difficult task. It's hard to know what problems will arise when modifying code, and in fact, you have to fix it assuming problems will occur. The stabilization period is quite long, and if not careful, it can truly fail.

However, there are cases where both the first and second methods are difficult: when there is no authority to modify existing code, or when the existing code is too complex to know what problems might arise if modified. Not having modification authority simply means using an external library. In this case, assuming problems will occur, additional code is haphazardly attached, and it's not clean. In terms of work speed, it's actually very good because you can use the existing code as is and quickly add additional features. However, it's not stable, and it's inefficient when implementing additional features due to the legacy.

So, our current education system continues to choose the third option. It's applying short-term remedies. It seems to be because there's no authority to modify the legacy when adding something new, and there's no overarching philosophy that permeates the education system.

I'm just rambling on today as thoughts come to mind, but this isn't just a problem with national history. What should be the goal of the core subjects of Korean, English, and Math in education? Discriminatory power for university admissions? I once saw an article stating that Korea's functional illiteracy rate is among the lowest in the world.
http://ideas0419.com/457[The Sad Portrait of South Korea: Functional Illiteracy Rate Among the Lowest in OECD A recent past survey result is once again gaining attention on social media. It is the fact that South Korea's functional illiteracy rate is among the lowest among OECD countries. High...ideas0419.com](http://ideas0419.com/457)
To be precise, it refers to reading comprehension. If students just memorize passages for exams, what need is there for reading comprehension? What Korean language education should aim for is reading comprehension, writing skills, and presentation skills. Listening well to others' writing and speech, and expressing oneself well – that is the fundamental value Korean language education should possess. Spelling and grammar, I believe, are perhaps secondary issues. Furthermore, literature is also intertwined with Korean language, but personally, I think it's closer to art than to the Korean language subject itself.

Literature, art, and music are fields that express emotions. In other words, they are functions of the right brain. What can encompass technology and SW is not technology, but engineering.

If I were to redefine things as they come to mind, it would be something like this: Korean Language (listening, speaking, writing), Mathematics, Natural Sciences (Biology/Chemistry/Space/Physics), Engineering (SW, Technology), Arts (Fine Arts, Literature, Music, Media Art), Industry (Economics, Finance, Marketing, Entrepreneurship), History (History of Technology/Science, History of Philosophy/Ideology/Religion, History of Nations/Wars/Politics), Foreign Languages (English/Chinese). As a side note, the advantage of SW is that it can divide a single piece of information into multiple dimensions or classify it through tagging. In fact, I believe that is the original essence of information (academics).

I recently heard talk of removing calculus education from mathematics. I oppose this. It's not because I'm against reducing children's study hours; I agree with reducing study hours. However, if one knows that calculus is used in almost all engineering and sciences, one would even argue that the grade level at which it's taught should be lowered. The important thing is not spending time teaching concepts, but the problem lies in teaching how to solve difficult problems. We need to broaden the level of abstraction in learning. If we understand how all knowledge intertwines, we will know what to study more deeply to solve a particular problem. If we are making things insanely difficult just to rank students and testing how few mistakes they make, then we have lost the essence of education.

Currently, that is the fundamental question of our education. I agree with the premise that SW fosters problem-solving abilities. However, I don't believe that memorizing what's taught in SW and doing well on exams can cultivate problem-solving abilities. SW is an empirical discipline. One must fail and experience enough to develop problem-solving skills. It's not a discipline where scores are counted by the number of correct answers, but one where overcoming mistakes is counted as a score.

In fact... this is the concept of differentiation (acceleration).

I've just rambled on today as thoughts came to mind. For future talent, perhaps we need to break the mold of subjects and engage in a grand discourse on education simultaneously.
