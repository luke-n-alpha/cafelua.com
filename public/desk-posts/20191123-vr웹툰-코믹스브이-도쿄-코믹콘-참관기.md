This business trip was a busy one, as ComicsV was exhibiting at Tokyo Comic-Con and also on the verge of launching its DPVR HMD in Japan, so I wanted to observe the market closely. Despite the packed schedule, I managed to make time to attend. My daughter told me not to go, calling the Japanese "bad guys"... While Korea-Japan relations aren't great these days, I believe there's no nationality barrier when it comes to loving comics, so I went anyway.

{{IMG:1}} VR Webtoon ComicsV appearing at Tokyo Comic-Con

After attending Comic-Con alone and wandering around, I learned a lot. What are ComicsV's strengths? What should we do?

**1. Comic-Con as a Blockbuster IP Event**
Tokyo Comic-Con is a huge event. It was absolutely packed with people. However, it felt more like a Marvel event than a Japanese manga event. It was like a giant shopping mall consuming large American IPs, perhaps? There were incredibly high-quality figures, life-sized cars and characters from comics... But the overall impression was that Comic-Con is being redefined as an event for major IPs rather than a comic festival. In the past, after experiencing Pirates of the Caribbean at Disneyland, I truly understood the value that large IP-based experiences bring. The upcoming release of Frozen 2 will likely be part of this market trend.

{{IMG:2}} It was incredibly crowded. Sony Pictures was about the only one showcasing technology.

The virtual reality industry I'm involved in also pursues wonderful experiences that don't actually exist. The power of blockbuster IP is immense in terms of immersive, good virtual stories. This seems different from the fancy merchandise brought by Line, Kakao, or character-driven brands like Kakao's Ryan, Line's Moon, or Kitty. IP, or rather, the unique power of a special story, lives and breathes through text and images, and people are willing to open their wallets to experience that content, which aligns with what VR/AR pursues.

These blockbuster IPs also seem to have a characteristic of being continuously spun off or collaborating to generate revenue. I even wondered if the winner-take-all market characteristic of platform businesses also applies to IP. Much like the platform strategy of internet companies, users can get lost within that single worldview without time to explore other stories. Villains and heroes, now used as proper nouns within a massive worldview, are examples of this.

Especially in this market, Disney seems poised to become an incredibly formidable company. Netflix may have interpreted VR merely as a bigger screen, but Disney's immersive experiences at Disneyland felt different. The experience at Disneyland didn't require an HMD or glasses; you rode attractions, and following the content's guided gaze, a pirate ship suddenly emerged from the water, and you entered a vast dome of the underwater world. The boat you were on sailed into a battle between pirate ships and warships seen in movies. Various immersive technologies, often used in robotics and VR/AR, are applied here. And as VR/AR technologies become more widespread, they will bring amusement parks closer to us.

However, conversely... I also worry that the emergence of such large IPs might create a polarization in the content market, making it harder to pursue content diversity.

**2. Small-scale Experience Content at a Ninja Cafe**
On my last business trip, I experienced a Maid Cafe in Akihabara, and this time, I stumbled upon a unique concept called a Ninja Cafe and decided to go in. It certainly wasn't cheap. 1400 yen for a glass of orange juice? After experiencing it, it was essentially a ninja version of a Maid Cafe... More than half of the customers were tourists. It was a very small two-story restaurant, too small to even call a small theater, with only about 7 small tables and 5 female ninjas working. They made each customer awkwardly shout "Nin-nin" and had a "ninja dance" during a showtime. If I were to compare it to something similar in Korea, the content structure might be similar to a bar with hostesses. However, it was different in that it was more wholesome and offered a unique story and cultural experience.

{{IMG:3}} This is called "Ninja-go." I couldn't take pictures of the ninjas.

If I talked about blockbuster IP earlier, this time it felt like content that isn't limited to a specific IP but is genre-flexible for commercial spin-offs, with a small amount of labor "ground in" (to put it nicely, high job creation). In any case, since it's a face-to-face service, it allowed for a more human touch, and I felt like I had an enjoyable experience.

**3. Potential for Combining Small-scale Experience Content with Immersive Technologies like VR/AR**
I connected these two experiences and thought about them. In the case of the Ninja Cafe, it's an offline, labor-intensive service model that isn't easy to scale up. But what if immersive technology were applied here?

There was a place in Tokyo, almost 5-star rated on Google Maps for the keyword "VR," called Ninja VR. Unfortunately, it was sold out, and I didn't have time to go, but I suspect it might be a place that connects such unique experiences with VR to create content.

In contrast, I think the mistake Korean VR rooms made was precisely that point. Consumers go to consume content, not VR technology. In Korea, there are various VR content production projects, exhibition support, and calls for regional specialization, but I haven't seen many complete content experiences that include the physical space. In fact, through this experience, I even imagined creating a "Joseon Cafe" in Jongno (but I will focus solely on VR webtoons). In Korea, the closest comparable space would be the Folk Village.

**4. What ComicsV Can Do Well**
And now, back to ComicsV... The best reactions at Comic-Con came from the artists. On the first day, I briefly stopped by and let one or two artists try it out. When I explained that it's a platform where anyone who draws 2D can upload content, and that it's great for high-quality art that was difficult to consume on small mobile screens, everyone agreed and showed great interest. While mobile webtoons are indeed gaining attention as next-generation content, high-quality print comics still represent the largest market in both Japan and North America.

So, that very day, I quickly created artist brochures and met with artists one by one the next day.

{{IMG:4}} Hastily made at a cafe that day, printed at Kinko's

As expected, the reaction was very positive. People who love comics recognized its value. With the help of my poor English and a translation app, I explained it to over 20 people individually, and I realized that people who love comics are the same all over the world.

It was tough, but it also made me realize that this is what we do best, and it was rewarding. In that sense, ComicsV seems more suited for Comic Market than Comic-Con. Although ComicsV's system still needs a lot of development, I felt confident that we are on the right track. When I return to Korea, I'll make sure to greet all the contacts I received and continue to develop diligently.
