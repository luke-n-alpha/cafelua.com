Although the full details of the incident have not yet been clearly elucidated, one confirmed fact is that a business registration number input field appeared for iTunes developer registration. This led to speculation ranging from pressure from the National Tax Service to discussions about value-added tax in global markets, which has been an ongoing issue. Rather than discussing who is right or wrong, I'd like to talk about how we should view this issue.

**1. When in Rome, do as the Romans do; if there's a transaction, there should be tax!**

That's a fair point. If global players generate income domestically but don't pay taxes unlike domestic businesses, then it's absolutely necessary to pursue and collect those taxes. This is because it prevents reverse discrimination against domestic businesses that are fulfilling their tax obligations.

Collecting taxes is as important as spending them. Beyond easy-to-tax salary earners or domestic businesses, taxes should certainly be collected from high-income earners and overseas businesses. Whenever taxes are discussed, what I most want to say is: don't pretend to reduce taxes; just collect them properly.

**2. Creative Economy? We need flexibility to protect one-person developers.**

One advantage of the internet industry is that it's easy to track who spent what down to the won. Therefore, collecting taxes is also relatively easy. How convenient would it be if taxes could be collected by just changing a few dozen lines of code in iTunes? However, we need to understand one characteristic of the internet industry that has changed from the past. Besides iTunes, there are other means to earn money through various overseas platforms, including Google AdSense and the App Store.

These global platforms can serve as a stepping stone, facilitating the global expansion of the domestic content industry. Therefore, the use of these platforms should clearly be encouraged to some extent.

A characteristic of the internet industry is that these global platforms can appear and disappear in an instant, and it's difficult to predict how they will evolve. While the taxes on the money earned by giant platforms in Korea are important, these platforms can also serve as a springboard for domestic businesses to go global.

Therefore, it's unreasonable to view the micro one-person businesses participating in this as falling within the existing framework of corporations and legal entities. Most importantly, if they are limited to existing corporate entities, the participation of students and developers employed by companies will be restricted, significantly narrowing the pool of developers.

In reality, the App Store market isn't easy enough to simply treat as a side job, but this would fundamentally block the entry of many developers with fresh ideas who try their hand at the App Store outside of their main work. And this is also the path most contrary to what the creative economy is pursuing.

**The problem must be solved with a prerequisite understanding of these two points. Neither position can truly be conceded. To cut it off like with a knife within the existing industrial framework without considering these aspects would truly be a 'stagnant economy' rather than a 'creative economy'.**

I believe the government is already giving this much thought, but I ask that you become a little busier with it.
