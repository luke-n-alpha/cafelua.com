Amidst the debate over whether the iPhone 5s and iOS 7 announcement was innovative or not, a piece of news emerged that countered the anti-Apple camp, which had been criticizing Apple. It's news about the iPhone breaking its sales record, which iPhone lovers will surely appreciate. Because of this, I've seen quite a few comments like, "See? The iPhone is an innovative product." I'd like to share how I interpret this latest record-breaking performance by the iPhone.

Honestly, I'm not sure if this is something to get angry about, but considering that Tim Cook was an SCM expert, this change at Apple is extremely natural. It's also obvious that the ROI from this approach would be much better than the ROI from high-quality innovation tailored to users who desire stronger and stronger stimuli. From a business perspective, what Apple urgently needed was not innovation, but expansion of its distribution network. And if they executed that, it's a very natural phenomenon for sales to break records. Furthermore, in terms of representing a significant change from the old Apple, this could be considered a greater innovation for Apple as a company, rather than just an improvement to the iPhone device itself.

**3. Sales Volume and Innovation Are Unrelated.**
I have only one thing to say: **Sales volume and innovation are unrelated**. An innovative company doesn't necessarily avoid failure, and a lack of innovation doesn't mean a company can't succeed. In other words, Apple has failed with innovative products in the past, and Samsung became a global company with products that weren't necessarily innovative. Innovation is meaningful for venture companies entering new markets or during business expansion. It's about disrupting an existing competitive market to create space for new players. In that sense, we must remember that Apple's iPhone was an expansion of its phone business. In the established duopoly of Mac vs. PC, not many people call the Mac innovative. Rather, a new player like Chrome OS is a better fit for innovation.

The reason for the Galaxy series' success isn't really innovation either. The initial innovation felt by users ended with the adoption of Google OS. What followed were simply faster and better Galaxy models. The same goes for Apple, which created the iPhone 3G. Even with iTunes on the subsequent iPhone 3GS, the innovation was in expanding the mobile phone business into the software business.

In fact, for Samsung and Apple, the winners in the smartphone market, innovation is no longer necessary. They do, however, need to stay sharp to avoid following in Nokia's footsteps.

Above all, users don't want innovative products. What users want are well-made products. The reality is that users don't know what they need and can't define their requirements. This is because they haven't thought deeply about it, so they don't present consistent demands. In response to this dilemma, manufacturers release innovative products that involve various experiments. And most of these innovative products end up as trash.

**4. I Dare Say Project Glass, Released Last Year, Is More Innovative Than the Future iPhone 6.**

I can state with certainty: Project Glass, released last year, is more innovative than the future iPhone 6. However, even if a new Project Glass product comes out, I would still choose the iPhone 4s. That's because what I need is an iPhone, more than Project Glass.

Let me ask again: Do you really want an innovative product? For your information, NASA is always making such cool products. However, if they were released as-is, consumers would surely call them trash.

I wish we could stop with the tiresome talk about innovation. If innovation is so great, go watch science fiction novels or animations. Everything is already there. The iPhone and iPad were already conceived long ago. The Galaxy didn't succeed because it was innovative; what moved history wasn't the innovative Tiger tank, but the mass-produced T34 tank.
