Last time, many people visited my post titled [iPhone 6 vs. Galaxy Note 4]. I didn't write the article to criticize Samsung, but many perceived it as defamation against Samsung. I suspect this misunderstanding arose because most visitors were either looking to buy a smartphone by comparing the two products or simply enjoyed the rivalry between them.

**1. First, a Buying Guide for iPhone 6 and Galaxy Note 4**
For reference, if you absolutely had to ask me to recommend between the iPhone 6 and Galaxy Note 4, I'd say the Galaxy Note 4 for those living in Korea, and for those living overseas, it depends on what phone you used previously. This is because I don't see a strong reason for those who enjoyed the advantages of the iPhone to switch to a Galaxy just for hardware specs. Adapting to Android anew would likely be quite bothersome. Of course, once you adapt to Android, you'll find the diverse range of devices appealing, and while the level of completion might be slightly lower than Apple's, you'll likely feel that interesting innovations are happening there first.

**3. For Those Who Want a Samsung vs. Apple Showdown**
Finally, for those who insist on a Samsung vs. Apple showdown, I'd say Samsung is the winner domestically, and Apple is the winner overseas. This is a metric that appears starkly in sales and profits, so there's not much more to say. **4. However, My True Feelings for Samsung and the Media: Software**
Let's return to the topic of my previous post. What I wanted to talk about was precisely this. The mobile ecosystem isn't just about hardware. What saved a cornered Samsung was certainly not Samsung's superior hardware technology. The Omnia wasn't significantly worse in hardware compared to the iPhone. What I wanted to convey in the previous topic was that Apple's keynote, including the iPhone 6, wasn't just about hardware. Apple's defensive range in the mobile ecosystem is wider than Samsung's. In fact, by slightly encroaching on Samsung's territory, Apple pushed Samsung into a defensive position with the power of its software and services. And Samsung, along with Google, successfully defended those lacking areas.
Therefore, to write an article about Apple's keynote, instead of [iPhone 6 vs. Galaxy Note 4], articles discussing the scope and meaning of the announcements made in the keynote would be more important and valuable. Furthermore, that table signifies **the crisis Samsung could face if its relationship with Google becomes difficult**, and also **Samsung's crisis when the center of gravity in the phone market shifts to software**. My opinion is that such events are indeed happening more and more. Of course, there's no need to try to defend every area like Apple. However, this is about where Samsung's weaknesses lie, and **my hope is that Korean media, when covering the mobile ecosystem, doesn't simply look at hardware.**

Therefore, **this means Samsung's score could be even lower if weighting is applied.**

**5. Why Don't the Media Pay Attention to Software?**
But why do the media and the public, who use cutting-edge software every day, not fully recognize the value of software?

**1) Software is inherently invisible**
I think it's because the business model of software isn't immediately visible like hardware. It's similar to how Warren Buffett viewed Silicon Valley as a bubble. Kakao, Facebook, and Line created enormous numbers of subscribers, but initially had no revenue, and instead, their investment (deficit) only grew. If judged by hardware company standards, this looked like they were continuously operating at a loss. Software revenue is generated not when the product reaches the user, but during the period of use. Thus, it's not easy to judge it based solely on the initial product.
Also, just as everyone perceives Naver as free, it doesn't really resonate with users, and only industry insiders understand it. Because the public doesn't feel it directly, it seems less attractive to the media. Instead, they only highly value free services like Kakao's VoiceTalk. This part seems to demand more professionalism from journalists in the software domain.

**2) Disruptive nature of existing advertisers' business models (Suspicion)**
This is somewhat of a suspicion, but for the media, whose main advertisers were mobile carriers and manufacturers, software companies that break their rules might not be welcome. Indeed, the iPhone achieved great success by breaking the rules of mobile carriers and entered the Korean market. News like Kakao offering free calls or Amazon giving away free phones might seem burdensome to the media's traditional advertisers.
**The popularization of software issues could be seen as a shift in power by traditional "smokestack industries" (manufacturing) and infrastructure (network) operators, so it might not be entirely welcome.** There are even talks of such friction between these two areas within companies like Samsung.
**Of course, this is merely a suspicion.**

**6. However, the Future is Software, the IT Market is Growing.**
I don't believe that hardware will disappear or cease to exist just because software is important. Rather, software can drive the growth of hardware, and new markets emerge, delivering new value to consumers, independent of hardware. As a result, the entire IT industry, including both software and hardware, expands. Only after software was combined with phones did they truly become smartphones, and this became a breakthrough for Samsung, which had been stagnant.
Now, I hope that the IT sections of the media will feature more stories about software and software ventures. Isn't that what speaks to Korea's future?
