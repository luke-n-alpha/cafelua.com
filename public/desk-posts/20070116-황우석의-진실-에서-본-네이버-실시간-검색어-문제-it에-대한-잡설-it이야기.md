Although there have been such problems before, it is clear that Naver's real-time search rankings have a very big problem.

Real-time search rankings themselves are not inherently bad.
On the contrary, by showing search rankings, it is an interesting and effective service that sensitively reflects real internet issues.

However, the problem is that it appears on Naver's top page, which gets 800 million views a day.
Furthermore, the 'commerciality' or 'truthfulness' of each search term cannot be judged by a machine.
Of course, Naver cannot judge it either.
Naver is an internet company, about half of which consists of engineers,
not a media company that employs journalists.
It merely relays news; it neither writes news nor investigates incidents.

Whether Hwang Woo-suk's story is true or false... I don't know.
I have neither the means nor the ability to verify it.

However, people, whether they want to or not, tend to see the real-time search terms on Naver's top page.
800 million times a day, to be precise.

Therefore, many people will click on them,
and once something appears on the top page, the more provocative and curious the title, the more clicks it will surge.

Previously, a certain fan club simultaneously entered a search term,
causing its real-time search ranking to skyrocket.

Such actions are clearly problematic.
If people intentionally and simultaneously enter 'Hwang Woo-suk's truth' to raise its ranking,

Naver's real-time search ranking will lose its objectivity.

So, should Naver remove this from the search rankings?
If they remove it, people will make a fuss about the removal,
and if they don't remove it, false or commercial information
will inevitably be intentionally spread.
If they don't remove anything at all, explicit words often rank high at night.
Would it be right not to remove those?

Many search terms in the existing search rankings have been like that.
Search rankings, which are supposed to inform people about issues,
are experiencing a reversal where they become an issue by manipulating the search rankings.

This can clearly be malicious.

Naver, in its own way, for objectivity,
sets principles and actually removes real-time search terms.
Naver must not lose its objectivity.
That is its duty as a portal,
and it will be a lonely battle.
Because numerous interest groups want to expose their intentions on the internet.
Among them, Naver's top page, which opens 800 million times a day, is nothing short of a golden land.

However, if Naver loses its objectivity,
it will merely become an internet tabloid, and its meaning as a portal serving as a medium for news (Naver itself is not a news organization) will fade.
Perhaps if the service were removed from the top page, it might even maintain objectivity.
However, it could lose its real-time search users entirely to competing portals.
Ultimately, to maintain objectivity, does it mean they have to go around individually verifying the truth and fact-checking?
If so, who can truly know the truth? Public opinion isn't always the truth, and if only the opinions of major media outlets are accepted, there's no meaning as an internet medium either...
It's a difficult situation all around...

From the inherent perspective of real-time search terms, the moment 'intent' from a specific group of the public or a company intervenes, it becomes malicious. At least a post like the one below is a clear manipulation of real-time search terms.

It's just another type of fraudulent click, where a specific search term is entered to rank one's own site higher. In fact, fraudulent clicks are recognized as a crime, and various portals like Google are making efforts to prevent them. Because the moment objectivity is lost, the value as a search engine declines.
Technically, blocking it is also not easy. The method of an unspecified multitude attacking from various IP ranges is similar to a DoS attack. Consequently, there's no choice but to remove them, similar to port filtering or IP filtering...

Why don't people realize that such actions are another form of 'manipulation' and 'social hacking'?
