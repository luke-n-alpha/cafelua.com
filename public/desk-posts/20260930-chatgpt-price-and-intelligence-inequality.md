Until now, intelligence was almost the only resource capital could not fully buy. You could buy some of it through education, but it took time, it was limited by your own mind, and above all you couldn't pass it on intact to your children. Regression to the mean was nature's ladder-repair mechanism. The dull child born to a rich family, the genius born to a poor one. The story of meritocracy kept some legitimacy thanks to that randomness. This is exactly what AI changes. Intelligence becomes an asset that is inherited, accumulated, and compounded. In a loop where intelligence earns capital and capital buys greater intelligence, there is no regression. I think one part of the Time Machine analogy doesn't fit. Wells's Morlocks fed the Eloi, so the classes needed each other. Exploitation is, at least, a relationship. One is the time lag between the models used internally and those sold externally; the other is the cost floor of the frontier. When value sits in the "good enough" range, open models catch up six months later and the ladder holds, but in domains where value arises only at the very front (research, markets, weapons), six months is everything. So I don't think this graph has one endpoint; it splits in two depending on the domain. A world where everyday intelligence becomes universal like literacy, while decisive intelligence stays with the few.

The printing press did spread knowledge in the end, but before that there was about a century of concentration. What I'm unsure of is not whether it will spread in the end this time too, but whether the transition hardens into something irreversible. Compounding gets crueler the longer it runs.

Then the story becomes clearer. And at this level "kicking away the ladder" isn't a metaphor; it's history that has already happened once. As Ha-Joon Chang laid out, Britain industrialized behind protectionism and then preached free trade, and of the countries that missed the industrialization train in the late 19th century, you can count on one hand those that caught up a century later. The industrialization gap soon became colonies. So the question is "how is it different this time," and I see two differences: one worse, one possibly better than expected.

The worse one. The periphery of the industrial age had bargaining cards: labor, resources, markets. And that labor was itself the ladder. Korea, Taiwan, and China all climbed by starting with cheap manufacturing labor and absorbing technology, but in a world where intelligence is produced by computation, that path closes. The inputs become chips, power, capital, and top-tier talent, and chips are bottlenecked on one EUV company and one or two foundries. You could replicate a steam engine in a decade or so by stealing blueprints and poaching engineers, but this bottleneck doesn't replicate like that. So the means of kicking away the ladder are already at work: chip export controls, weight controls. And a country that imports intelligence is not like a country that imported machines and took them apart. Using ChatGPT doesn't teach you how to build ChatGPT. A category of intelligence-importing nations paying permanent rent will emerge, like energy importers.

The possibly better one. It has always been the runner-up that puts the ladder back. The U.S. talked about free trade only after it became number one; before that it tried to break Britain's rents. Look at who is scattering open weights across the world right now. The cheapest weapon for breaking the leader's rents is free intelligence, so as long as a gap exists, the runner-up has an incentive to keep building ladders. The problem is that this ladder reaches only the "good enough" range, and the six-month lag in decisive domains still belongs to the leader.

So the end of this graph is probably not two blocs and the rest, but three kinds of nations: those that make intelligence, those that hold at least one of its bottlenecks, and those that buy it. That Korea is still in the second category is a comfort for now, but bottlenecks move within a generation. I think this kicking-away will be crueler than in the industrial age. Back then, even if you kicked others away, you still needed their labor and couldn't abandon them entirely. This time that need is gone.

Yes, and at the national level that's even scarier. An individual's compounding is cut off by death, but nations don't die. Just transpose Piketty's r > g. The moment the return on intelligence exceeds the growth rate of society as a whole, the gap diverges without redistribution. Capital at least has inheritance tax; intelligence has no equivalent at all. Add the recursive loop of intelligence designing better intelligence, and it is not compounding but compounding on compounding.

Still, I don't think this compounding is infinite. Capital compounds only while returns hold, and intelligence likewise meets friction in two places. One is contact with reality. New knowledge must ultimately pass through experiments, the physical world, and time-consuming verification, and no amount of purchased intelligence can run that in parallel. There are domains, like new drugs, materials, and geopolitics, where the speed at which the world answers is the ceiling. The other is that intelligence, unlike capital, isn't fungible. Money is money wherever you put it, but an edge in protein folding doesn't automatically transfer to semiconductor processes. So divergence will happen, but not uniformly; it will split at different speeds in different domains.

But for these frictions to be a comfort, one condition must hold: those who fall behind must be able to get on board within that friction time. The cruelty of compounding lies not in the interest rate but in when you start. Even at the same rate, start ten years late and you'll never catch up. So I fully agree that these few years decide not the size of the gap but whether the gap is the reversible kind. In industrialization that window was decades; how much shorter it gets when compounding is stacked on compounding is the part I truly don't know.
