The fifth keyword is the stagnation of mobile's quantitative growth. This year will be the year when a significantly different pattern from the existing mobile growth begins to unfold. Since the advent of the iPhone, the domestic smartphone market, which had been growing rapidly, has ceased its quantitative growth. I felt it was similar to the era of Windows PC proliferation. With the advent of Windows PCs, computers that were difficult to handle without basic developer-level knowledge in the past quickly spread to homes and offices. As Pentium CPUs and the internet began to spread, Windows PCs became such innovative devices that they felt completely different from PCs of the DOS era. The Windows game StarCraft explosively increased the gaming population. However, with Windows XP as its peak, the atmosphere began to change from Windows ME. Demand for new OSs and features weakened, and no game emerged after StarCraft and MMORPGs that would change the landscape of the gaming market. Meanwhile, Naver established itself as an internet company. Thus, the PC market became consolidated, and users no longer craved new innovations. And from then on, the need for faster CPUs or higher graphics also decreased, and competition in service quality began. I believe the same thing will happen in mobile, and this year feels like the time when Windows XP reached its peak.

**1. Manufacturers: The End of Smartphone Spec Competition**
As I mentioned earlier, if we consider it a re-enactment of the past PC market, Pentium 4 is the current quad-core era. From Pentium 4 onwards, the need for speed competition significantly decreased. Currently, the speed difference between Galaxy S3 and Galaxy S4 is not significantly felt. Some say that innovation disappeared from Android after Gingerbread and from iOS after 4.0. However, it's not that improvements aren't happening. Speeds are still increasing, and new and novel features are being added to OSs. The problem is that the definition of the attributes of a smartphone as a device has now become fixed and mature. Chimpanzees are not the ancestors of humans. Chimpanzees are perfect organisms that have established themselves through evolutionary competition. Similarly, the smartphone, as a device itself, has completed its evolution. Very large changes, on the contrary, would break the inherent attributes of the device itself, making it inconvenient. Examples of this are cases where the smartphone experience is directly applied to cameras or watches. Just as Windows tried to transfer the PC experience directly to mobile, and thus collapsed so helplessly against Apple. This phenomenon inevitably leads to an unfavorable situation for market leaders. Unless they possess core technology and defend based on it, the specs of latecomers will not differ significantly. The crisis of Samsung Electronics, which once excelled in mobile phones, was due to the same reason. Even then, Samsung was making the best mobile phones in the world, but as an era arrived where technological differences felt negligible to users, the entire mobile phone industry became a red ocean. And in the smartphone market, Lenovo, ZTE, and Huawei are closely catching up, raising concerns about a re-enactment of that time. Apple is also in a similar situation, and Google's strategy of focusing on practical gains without concentrating on phone manufacturing seems to shine.

**2. Telecommunications: LTE Saturation, Deepening Concerns for Telecom Companies**
The telecommunications market is no different. Recalling the past PC market, it started with the widespread adoption of 100mb fiber optic internet. As 100mb internet became widespread from 10mb internet, speed competition was no longer possible. From then on, it devolved into a fierce subsidy war. If 10mb internet was 3G, then 100mb internet is LTE. Although there are media leaks suggesting that LTE-A technology will be released soon, the actual possibility doesn't seem very high. It's similar to why gigabit wired technology exists but isn't widely adopted. This is because the effect of attracting users is not very high compared to the investment cost of increasing speed. They would need to recoup the investment made in LTE, and LTE still requires further investment. However, subscribers will soon reach saturation this year. Few countries have seen LTE spread this rapidly. Whatever the case, this situation will present telecom companies with the challenge of finding new revenue streams, and it will lead to a different scenario than when they relied solely on LTE.

**3. Services: The Entrenchment of Top Mobile Apps**
Kakao and Naver, these two brands have virtually dominated the most critical areas of the non-gaming sector, and changes in their rankings are not easy. In search, Naver relatively successfully defended against Google's mobile offensive, while Daum's influence was reduced to a level similar to Google's. In other social network areas, the influence of Facebook and Twitter from abroad is significant, but in the core real-time communication sector, Kakao succeeded, effectively emerging and establishing itself as the domestic powerhouse of social networks. Rapid changes in their rankings are now unlikely to occur. As mentioned earlier, as the platform itself matures, the cost of teaching users new experiences has significantly increased. Beyond the competitiveness of the service, specific services have already become synonymous with a genre or field, solidifying their brand position. To overcome this, a rather protracted and tedious battle would have to be fought. Just as heroes emerge in troubled times, there were relatively many situations for heroes to emerge in the early days of smartphones. However, such possibilities have gradually decreased, so the likelihood of a new mobile powerhouse emerging like a dark horse in Korea from 2014 onwards is not as high as one might think, and the mobile ventures that have sprung up like mushrooms will likely be sorted out. For starters, unlike before, users now only look for and install the apps they used to use, and don't enjoy experimenting with new apps. Of course, there's an exception: games. Because games are a genre that constantly seeks new stimuli and experiences.

**4. But there are still emerging markets. Korea to leap globally**
But it's too early to be disappointed. Having achieved mobile saturation and created a mature experience earlier than any other country in the world, this experience provides a foundation to relatively easily dominate newly emerging markets. Facebook's bloodless entry into most countries was likely also due to its growth through fierce competition in the United States. There are countries rapidly entering the information society, including China, Southeast Asia, and Africa. These countries will be the battlegrounds of the smartphone market. Because those places are still in troubled times. In 2014, I hope to see more global mobile leaders emerge from Korea.
