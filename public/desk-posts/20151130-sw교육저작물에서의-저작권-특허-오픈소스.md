Nowadays, anyone can create an app and upload it to a marketplace. Moreover, with the current craze for software education, we are entering an era where anyone can create software. While not everyone knows the specifics, most people are aware that software is protected by copyright. However, even corporate software engineers or those looking to start a startup often get confused about the actual scope of copyright protection and what exactly can be created and how. Even more so, children just starting to create something with software, or teachers without business experience, probably don't know much about software copyright. Therefore, I'd like to discuss common mistakes and what is permissible.

**1. The Boundary Between Legal and Illegal is Distribution, Not Duplication**
One common misconception is that illegal software duplication is illegal. However, in reality, duplication itself is not illegal. For software for which you have acquired usage rights, such as through purchase or subscription, duplication for personal use is not illegal. This is because the scope of the usage rights does not change. However, if you distribute it to unauthorized individuals, the distributor is actually committing an illegal act. Furthermore, a user who uses a copied version is an illegal software user. Therefore, the boundary between legal and illegal is determined not by the act of duplication, but by distribution, which expands the scope of use.

**2. Using Software Outside of Designated Locations is Illegal**
It is illegal to take software that was freely duplicated and used at school or work and use it personally at home. However, it is often somewhat unclear whether this constitutes an extension of academic work or study. Therefore, while it may not be strictly enforced, using it outside of the designated physical location is generally understood to be illegal.

**3. Illegally Taking, Processing, and Distributing Free Content Published Online is Illegal**
If you unauthorizedly take free content published on the internet and create new software, it is generally illegal. When I used to work on webtoons, I once received an inquiry from a confident student who wanted to crawl webtoon information and create a new app to provide it. That is illegal. Webtoons themselves are copyrighted works, but thumbnails and title lists are also considered copyrighted works. Furthermore, these free services are generally provided for free through advertising or other business models. If you ignore this and create a new business that directly accesses content via URLs, it could constitute interference with business operations. In fact, in the case of Seoul Bus, Yoo Jun-wan, being a high school student, was able to bravely create it. At the time, local governments charged money for providing traffic information. Of course, since URLs on the internet are public, using them itself is not illegal. Also, as mentioned earlier, personally possessing crawled data is not illegal. The problem arises when you distribute it to disrupt a service or access it through abnormal channels, causing harm to the service. The most legitimate way is to develop using publicly available APIs, such as OpenAPI. Even in this case, you must check the mandatory requirements enforced by the respective developer center. For Naver or Daum, I recall they required adding 'Powered by ****'. Generally, due to limited resources, these aren't checked individually, but if you violate the terms of use, the API service can be cut off or a lawsuit filed, and you'll have no grounds to complain, so keep that in mind. I've even seen students from Naver's 'next' school not know this and fail to comply.

**4. Distributing Games Outside of Marketplaces is Illegal**
Marketplaces like Google Play or Naver App Store have acquired the status of open game marketplaces and, with authority delegated by the Game Rating and Administration Committee, allow games to be published without separate rating reviews. However, distributing games without a rating review through other means is illegal. Without establishing a corporation, distribution outside of marketplaces is virtually impossible. Distributing through cafes or blogs is, of course, illegal. Even KakaoTalk or email distribution is, strictly speaking, illegal. Of course, this absolutely does not mean that KakaoTalk and emails should be intercepted to prevent this.

**5. The Ambiguity of Games in Services for Sharing Learning Outcomes**
When games are distributed on sites where learning outcomes are shared for software education, it could, strictly speaking, be considered a violation. Sites like Scratch or Entry are also not exempt from this. Strictly speaking, if a site distributes games without a rating, it could be flagged with 'warning.kr' and would have no real grounds for objection. However, I certainly agree that this would be an excessive measure. I believe that sites created for learning and sharing purposes are difficult to classify as distribution in terms of their intent. Furthermore, even if they haven't acquired marketplace status, they can be considered to be managed more strictly than marketplaces due to their educational purpose.

**6. Content Created Solely for Learning Purposes is Legal**
While excessive distribution could be an issue, content created for learning purposes generally doesn't pose a major problem even if it uses some images or IP from commercial content. This means that even if you "pump" images from the internet to create a game, it's generally not a big problem if it's for learning. However, the moment you create it well and upload it to a marketplace, it becomes distribution and could be illegal, so caution is advised. And, of course, it must be separated from companies that create and sell educational materials, as they are used for commercial purposes. I understand that this, too, isn't always a clear-cut distinction depending on the use case.

**7. Derivative Works are Copyrighted Works in Themselves, But Copyright Issues with the Original Work Must Be Resolved**
A derivative work is recognized as a separate copyrighted work if new elements are added to an existing work, making it an independent work. However, copyright issues with the original work must be resolved. Generally, content distribution platforms are likely to treat derivative works as illegal if there is no specific mention or agreement regarding them.

**8. Patents: If You Have a New Idea, Don't Hesitate, Just Build It. If It's for Learning, Don't Worry About It. Patent Lawsuits Only Happen When There's a Significant Threat.**
Patents are a separate concept from copyrights. While copyright grants rights to expression, patents grant rights to ideas. Cases often referred to as 'copycat' usually involve infringement of an idea. However, patents, which are required to legally protect an idea, are complex, and proving infringement when it occurs is also not easy. Therefore, they are often used defensively, or not even registered due to cost. In cases of fast-lifecycle software, minor infringements tend to be less of a concern. What is clear is that software is protected by patents and copyrights. However, if you try to search for every patent for every new idea, you might get exhausted before even creating the service. If you feel you have a good idea that doesn't exist yet, I recommend just building it. If it's for learning, just make it. Patent lawsuits only happen when there's a threat, because lawsuits cost money.

**9. Open Source: Like an Unsharpened Sword**

Today, open source generally does not impose an obligation to distribute the source code merely by using it. The obligation to distribute the source code arises when it is modified.
