In 2012, the issue of smart TVs in the TV market heated up considerably. And, I heard that the 2013 CES was dominated by discussions about 4K resolution UHD. It seems like the TV market is bustling, scrambling to find new growth engines. While I expected manufacturers to focus heavily on smart TVs for a while, it seems 2013 is seeing a clash centered around UHD. Just as I primarily discussed smart TVs from a service perspective, this time I'd like to talk a bit about UHD. **1. UHD Broadcasting, Will It Really Take Off?** The demand for high-resolution broadcasting is constant. The problem lies in production and sourcing costs. It's questionable whether many production companies, who already struggle to create Full HD content, will be able to produce UHD broadcasts. Ultimately, only a few or large production companies will be able to produce it. What's particularly expected to emerge are high-quality blockbuster dramas, music programs, and documentaries. In fact, this phenomenon already occurred when HD was introduced. I think this aspect will become even more pronounced. And, the increased unit cost will likely further boost the demand for the paid broadcasting market.

**2. Let's Not View UHD Technology Solely from a Broadcasting Perspective.** Many tend to view the increase in UHD resolution primarily from a broadcasting perspective, but I see it from a slightly different angle. What Steve Jobs talked about regarding Retina displays was 'text readability.' And UHD, too, sees such an increase in resolution that it can be called the Retina of TVs. Ultimately, this means that from merely transmitting video information, it gradually becomes useful for transmitting text information, like on the web. If you transmit a Full HD source, you can allocate the remaining 3/4 area to apps or the web without hindering viewing. In other words, UHD gains more significance as a tool for smart TVs, which became a hot topic in 2012. I actually participated in TV app planning, and text readability was a very important issue to consider. Not all TVs are 1080p. Therefore, we had to consider 720p levels, and in trying not to obscure the broadcast or restrict viewing, the usable resolution actually dropped to less than half of that. Consequently, we couldn't even secure 10 lines of text to display. However, with UHD, the story can change. (Of course, if the text becomes too small depending on viewing habits, it's still equally unreadable...)

**3. UHD, a Better Fit for Smart TVs** In conclusion, UHD is very welcome news for smart TVs. Even when using PIP, spatial constraints for information delivery have disappeared, and it's now possible to provide a more elegant app and web experience. How this expanded space can be utilized will ultimately be up to those who create services, but I wanted to convey that UHD technology should not be viewed solely based on broadcasting and video. Of course, the addition of UHD doesn't immediately mean that smart TVs have all the elements needed for a boom. Rather, I believe the most important point lies in the 'value chain' that smart TVs themselves will create, more so than the technological environment. Currently, it's still regrettable that no company with the leadership to drive this is visible. Will the 2014 CES reveal such a company? I look forward to it.
