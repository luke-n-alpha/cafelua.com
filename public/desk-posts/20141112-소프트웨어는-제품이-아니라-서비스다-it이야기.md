**1. We know software is important, but the persistent crisis theories and their causes**
Crisis theories are circulating about traditional manufacturers like Samsung Electronics and Hyundai Motor, which formed the backbone of Korean industry, and the cause is being sought in software.
It is already a well-known fact that software constitutes a significant and growing portion of the manufacturing industry, so Korean manufacturers would not have overlooked the importance of software.
Samsung Electronics undertook massive recruitment of software personnel and even developed and distributed its own OS like Tizen, but why do the crisis theories about Korean manufacturing persist?
I believe the main reason for this is not limited to Korean manufacturing but stems from a widespread '**misconception of software as a product**' throughout Korea.

**2. The misconception of software as a product**
**1) The misconception that software is cheap**
Nowadays, some people associate software with Naver and Google, but many still think of Hancom Office's Hangul and AhnLab's V3 when they hear "software." While it might be due to the internet industry's origins in the software industry not being widely known, this perception of software still seems to lead many to understand it as something tangible, recalling floppy disks or CD-ROMs.
If one only considers the price of such tangible media, a software company might be seen as a business that makes a lot of money with just a small idea—a high-value-added industry, to put it positively, but negatively, as thieves who make infinite money after creating something once. This also leads to less guilt about copying.
However, while it's true that software can be started with low costs, the idea that creating good software also costs little is clearly a misconception.
In the past, when discussing the advanced automotive industry, people talked about the high complexity, citing 20,000 parts in a car. But currently, the software in a car boasts 1 million lines of code, 50 times the number of parts. Furthermore, with the recent emergence of concepts like smart cars, this scale is growing exponentially, to the point where it's said that cars run on software.
Considering that complexity increases with scale in software, and it requires high-skilled labor costs, it's easy to see that the idea software requires little investment is a misconception.
Therefore, if you want software competitiveness, sufficient investment must be made. This point seems to be easily misunderstood by both those adopting and purchasing software. They might think, "Isn't it enough if it just works?" but even for the same function, software can have enormous cost differences depending on its quality.

**2) From the perspective of traditional industries, software is a cost.**
Furthermore, to maintain good quality, software requires continuous updates after a product is released. Therefore, if software is considered a product, updates are a cost, and that cost should be included in the product's price.
From the perspective of companies that need to adopt or integrate software, software is merely a cost incurred upon purchase or a continuous consumption of computing resources.
Moreover, as the proportion of software in products steadily increases, product prices remain the same while software costs rise, leading to a decrease in margins. This creates a very undesirable situation for traditional industries. This implies that the smartification and software-ization of products require a complete paradigm shift for existing industries, including manufacturing, to survive.

**3. Software is a service industry.**
**1) An Industry Centered on Customers, Not Company Categories**
How would a company's business change if software were viewed as a service rather than a product?
Amazon, already well-known, produces tablets and phones, but it does not derive profit from selling these devices; instead, it generates revenue based on content sales and online shopping.
My example does not mean that manufacturers like Samsung Electronics should enter the internet industry. Rather, demanding that current large manufacturers suddenly enter e-commerce, content businesses, or internet advertising industries is not only unreasonable but can also create co-existence issues with venture companies.
The internet is merely the most crucial infrastructure technology that has transformed the software industry into a service industry by ensuring the connection between customers and developers.
Amazon's entry into groceries, Google's entry into automobiles, and Apple's entry into the payment market clearly show that they are not confined to PC or smartphone internet businesses.
The difference with these software companies is that they operate in a software-driven manner, centered around the customer.
A characteristic of software companies is that they start from their existing products or services, identify and solve customer needs, and from there, discover value and generate profit.
I believe Korean manufacturers still have the opportunity to act like other software companies, in that they possess customer touchpoints.

**2) The software business begins the moment it reaches the customer.**
Furthermore, software companies, where hidden customer demands become the market, prioritize customer touchpoints and customer data.
For this reason, software companies engage in various marketing activities, such as distributing devices for free or encouraging software installation, to acquire customer data.
"Buy our product because we've included paid software for free at our expense." Isn't this the exact opposite approach to what manufacturers take? I was surprised recently to see LG Smart TV equipped with WebOS and a more refined UX than before, but I was soon disappointed to see it was more expensive simply because it was a smart TV. If it were a software company, they would have released it cheaper and actively encouraged customers to try out the smart features. I realized they still wanted to charge for the product by providing those features to customers.
For a software company, the business begins the moment the software is installed and the product reaches the customer, and every action the customer takes to experience the service means the business is ongoing.
These software companies, whose software has reached customers, continue to compete even after reaching them. Although it varies by case, it's possible to switch to other software with just one click, and a low switching cost is also a characteristic.
Therefore, they constantly strive and compete not to be abandoned by customers even while providing the service. If software is considered a product, the moment the product reaches the customer is the end of the business, and everything after that becomes after-sales service, or post-purchase cost. This is precisely where the potential for extreme differences in customer satisfaction lies, even if a software company and a non-software company offer the same product. And that customer satisfaction will soon manifest as a difference in corporate profit.

**4. Declining Hardware Value, Increasing Software Value with Technological Advancement**
If a manufacturer wishes to transform into a software company, what it needs to strengthen is not the product but the service, and at the heart of service is software.
People no longer pay for products. The era is transforming into one where people experience products, and if the experience has value, they open their wallets according to that experienced value. Cloud services, on-demand services, and the sharing economy, which allow users to select only the necessary functions in real-time and pay only for what they use based on the internet, already indicate that this era has begun.
In an extreme case, futurist Jeremy Rifkin even talks about an era where product profits could become zero. This assumption is based on extreme efficiency through the utilization of information, meaning the value of software will not only remain but can be said to be an era where it is maximized.

**5. Korea's Shortcomings: Time to Re-evaluate the Perception of Software Value**
Recently, I saw comments on a news article about people lining up for the iPhone 6, and many comments, ranked high by recommendations, expressed not understanding why people would wait so uncomfortably and arduously for something they could get easily or cheaper by waiting.
Seeing those comments, I felt regret and concern that many still compare only CPU clock speeds and camera megapixels, failing to recognize intangible values like software.
The value of software is invisible, so it is determined by the value derived from people's experiences.
Furthermore, even the same experience provides a different feeling and its value changes depending on when, how, and to whom it is supplied.
If one does not understand this software-driven competition, one might not understand why profits differ despite offering the same functions, and software might appear, as mentioned earlier, as if software companies are making excessive money without much effort.
We can see similar examples offline: the business and service of a luxury bag are said to begin the moment one queues at a department store to buy it. The act of queuing itself sells the psychological satisfaction of proving one is different from others. The profit difference between Starbucks and vending machine coffee also arises from this. The act of queuing to buy an iPhone is already paying for a psychological product related to the culture Apple is selling.
This is even easier to find in the digital software industry.
IPTV offers paid VODs that become free after a certain period. Webtoons allow users to preview next week's comics for a fee, and in games, one can purchase game time or upgrade in-game abilities for a fee.
These many examples can be said to be instances of software-driven services that create different values by supplying the same product differently, and these differences come from meticulous customer analysis.
**An era where money is paid for the experience of software, not the product of hardware,** is one aspect of a software-centric society, and it is the era we must prepare for.

In this area, software is produced and utilized, but both clients and developers still seem to treat and trade software like a product. Therefore, I believe that improving this area is urgent to restore the inherent competitiveness of software. {{IMG:1}}
