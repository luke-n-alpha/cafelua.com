When I was six, I once read a picture book. More than anything, the cover of that book
caught my eye.
 A child standing alone on a tiny star. Back then I thought about how lonely that child must be.
But when I came to read the book at eight, I realised the child was not
lonely. He was living with a rose that had four thorns.
 Absurdly enough, it was that which grew my dream of space. My longing for that far, far
universe. Our house was in the countryside, in a hamlet of a dozen or so households. We kept an orchard,
and the intercity bus came and went perhaps twice a day — that kind of quiet country place.
 Perhaps that is why the stars showed so well from our rooftop. Looking at those stars that seemed
close enough to take in my hand, I wanted to go there.
 It was not like it is now. Numbers did not interest me much then.
 But in any case, now I really have come out into space. As a labourer on a planet called Delta 21 in the
Magellanic Cloud, admittedly. And now I remember the little prince's star better by the number B612 — the
number given it by a Turkish astronomer in a fine suit who first found it through a telescope
in 1920.
 But I still have that book. Packed away tight in my travelling case, of course.
 What I do is extract the resources to be carried back to Earth from this planet. I ride a
working robot called a Labour down into the deep underground of the asteroid, take out ore and gas, and
haul it back up. This is very hard work. There is no clean air to
breathe. On this planet air of any kind never existed in the first place.
 What is more, inside the cramped, hot Labour you chew space rations that taste like gum, and you deal with
your own urine and waste in there as well. The Labour has to be treated as something precious.
It belongs to the company and it is extremely expensive. On the day you damage a Labour badly by
mistake, several months of wages simply come to nothing.

 The small thick window of the shower room, the one that opens onto space, is within reach of my hand.
The window is clouded over by the steam of the shower. I wipe it clean with my hand
and put my face to it and look out. The cold feel of the window and my exhaustion come over
my face together.
 Space stretching out without end... and nebulae, beautiful and nothing else, giving off light in five colours.
The space I longed for as a child is now in a place I could touch if I put out my hand.

 And then wait until that star comes right above your head. When at that moment some

 And if that happens, do me a kindness. Do not leave me sunk in this grief

 But there is a problem. It is already two hundred years since Monsieur Saint-Exupéry left this
